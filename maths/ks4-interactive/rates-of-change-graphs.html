Rates of Change Graphs

A rate of change tells you how fast one quantity changes compared with another: kilometres per hour, litres per minute, pounds per hour, °C per minute. On a graph, the rate of change is the gradient. This page is about reading real-life graphs and saying what their gradients (and, for velocity, their areas) mean.

GCSE subject content: interpret the gradient of a straight-line graph as a rate of change; plot and interpret graphs in real contexts, including simple kinematic problems involving distance, speed and acceleration; (Higher) interpret the gradient at a point on a curve as the instantaneous rate of change; apply the concepts of average and instantaneous rate of change (gradients of chords and tangents); interpret areas under velocity–time graphs

Before you start: a quick recap

  • Gradient = rise ÷ run: how far the graph goes up for each 1 across. Uphill is positive, downhill negative, flat is 0. Revise it on Linear Graphs.
  • Speed = distance ÷ time. Units come from the division: km ÷ hours = km/h, metres ÷ seconds = m/s.
  • Time conversions: 30 minutes = 0.5 hours, 15 minutes = 0.25 hours, 20 minutes = ⅓ hour. Divide minutes by 60 to get hours (see Unit Conversion).
  • The method for drawing tangents and estimating areas under curves is on the companion page Gradients, Tangents and Area under Graphs. This page focuses on what the answers mean in real life.

1 · Gradient means "per"

A graph of cost against miles, volume against minutes or pay against hours has a gradient with units: the y-units per x-unit. That's the rate of change. Pick a situation, then drag the two plum points along the line to draw the gradient triangle anywhere. The answer is always the same, because the line is straight: the rate is constant.

Say what it means, with units. "The gradient is 1.8" scores less than "the cost goes up by £1.80 per mile". A negative gradient means the quantity is going down: −12.5 % per hour means the battery loses 12.5% every hour. The y-intercept usually means the starting value (a fixed charge, the water already in the tank, the battery at the start).

2 · Distance–time graphs

A distance–time graph shows how far someone is from their starting point as time goes on. The gradient is distance ÷ time, which is speed. Press play and watch the cyclist on the road above the graph.

  • Steeper line = faster.
  • Horizontal line = not moving (stationary): time passes but the distance stays the same.
  • Line going down = coming back towards the start (a return journey).

Try it: read a speed

Exam trap: minutes on the time axis. If the time axis is in minutes and the question wants km/h, convert the time to hours first. 9 km in 30 minutes is 9 ÷ 0.5 = 18 km/h, not 9 ÷ 30 = 0.3. Also read the change in distance for that section only, not the distance from home.

3 · Average speed for a whole journey

The average speed for a journey is the total distance divided by the total time. On a distance–time graph it's the gradient of the straight line from the start to the end (the plum dashed line). It is not the average of the speeds, because you usually spend longer on the slower part.

4 · Velocity–time graphs

A velocity–time graph shows how fast something is going at each moment. (Velocity is speed in a particular direction; at GCSE you can treat it as speed.) Now the gradient is velocity ÷ time, which is the acceleration: how quickly the speed changes, in metres per second per second, written m/s².

  • Sloping up: speeding up (positive acceleration).
  • Horizontal: constant speed (acceleration 0). Not stopped! Stopped is a horizontal line along the time axis, at velocity 0.
  • Sloping down: slowing down (deceleration, a negative acceleration).

Distance is the area under the graph Higher

If you travel at a constant 20 m/s for 10 s, you go 20 × 10 = 200 m. On the velocity–time graph that's a rectangle 10 wide and 20 tall: its area is the distance. The same is true for any shape: the area between the graph and the time axis is the distance travelled. For straight-line graphs, split the area into rectangles and triangles (area of a triangle = ½ × base × height), or use one trapezium. Tick "shade the area" above to see it split up. For curved velocity–time graphs you estimate the area with strips: that method is on Gradients, Tangents and Area under Graphs.

Don't mix the two graphs up. On a distance–time graph, the gradient is speed and a flat line means stopped. On a velocity–time graph, the gradient is acceleration, a flat line means constant speed, and (Higher) the area is the distance. Always read the label on the y-axis first.

5 · Filling containers: rates in other units

Water flows into each container at a constant rate (the same number of cm³ every second). How fast does the depth rise? In a narrow part, a little water makes the level shoot up: the depth–time graph is steep. In a wide part, the same water spreads out and the level creeps up: the graph is shallow. Where the width changes gradually, the gradient changes gradually, so the graph is a curve.

Choose a container. Before you press play, predict the shape of its graph.

Litres per minute

A volume–time graph for a tank: gradient = litres per minute. Positive while it fills, negative while it drains, zero when the tap is off.

Pounds per hour

Pay against hours worked: the gradient is the hourly rate. A steeper section part-way along could be overtime at a higher rate.

Conversion graphs

Euros against pounds: the gradient is the exchange rate, euros per pound. A straight line through the origin is direct proportion.

6 · Average rate: the gradient of a chord Higher

Many real graphs are curves: the rate keeps changing. A car pulling away goes faster and faster; a cup of tea cools quickly at first, then more slowly. The average rate of change between two times is the gradient of the straight line joining those two points on the curve. That line is called a chord. It's exactly the "average speed" line from section 3: total change ÷ total time.

7 · Instantaneous rate: the gradient of a tangent Higher

What is the car's speed at exactly t = 6 seconds? Not on average over a time interval, but at that instant: the speedometer reading. That's the instantaneous rate of change. On the graph it's the gradient of the tangent: the straight line that just touches the curve at that point, going in the same direction as the curve there.

Why does that work? Make the chord shorter and shorter. Slide "chord width" towards 0: the plum chord swings round until it lies along the orange tangent, and the average rate over a tiny interval becomes the rate at that instant. Drag the point to move along the curve.

Common mistakes with curves

  • Using the curve instead of the tangent. Rise ÷ run between two points on the curve gives an average rate (a chord), not the rate at one instant.
  • Dividing y by x at the point. At t = 6 the car has gone 21.6 m, and 21.6 ÷ 6 = 3.6 m/s is its average speed since the start, not its speed at t = 6 (which is 7.2 m/s).
  • A short triangle. Choose two points far apart on the tangent: small reading errors matter less.
  • Forgetting the sign and units. A cooling curve has a negative gradient: "−2.1 °C per minute" means the temperature is falling by 2.1 °C each minute.

Worked examples

A taxi fare graph is a straight line from (0, 3) to (10, 21), with miles across and £ up. Interpret the gradient and the intercept.
Gradient: rise ÷ run = (21 − 3) ÷ (10 − 0) = 18 ÷ 10 = 1.8.
Units: £ per mile. So the fare goes up by £1.80 for every mile travelled.
Intercept: £3 when the distance is 0: a fixed charge of £3 before the taxi moves.
Amir cycles 9 km from home in the first 30 minutes, stops for 15 minutes, then cycles home in 45 minutes. Find his speed on each moving section in km/h.
Out: 9 km in 30 min = 0.5 h. Speed = 9 ÷ 0.5 = 18 km/h.
Stopped: horizontal line, speed 0.
Home: 9 km in 45 min = 0.75 h. Speed = 9 ÷ 0.75 = 12 km/h. The line slopes down because he's getting closer to home.
Trap: 9 ÷ 30 = 0.3 is in km per minute. Multiply by 60 to get km/h: 0.3 × 60 = 18 ✓
A driver goes 30 km at 60 km/h, then 30 km at 30 km/h. Find the average speed for the whole journey.
Time for each leg: 30 ÷ 60 = 0.5 h; 30 ÷ 30 = 1 h.
Totals: distance 60 km, time 1.5 h.
Average speed = 60 ÷ 1.5 = 40 km/h.
Not 45 km/h! (60 + 30) ÷ 2 = 45 ignores that the driver spent twice as long going slowly.
A car accelerates from rest to 20 m/s in 8 s, travels at 20 m/s for 10 s, then brakes to rest in 5 s. Find its acceleration and deceleration.
Speeding up: gradient = (20 − 0) ÷ 8 = 2.5 m/s². Its speed goes up 2.5 m/s every second.
Cruising: horizontal line, acceleration 0 (constant speed, not stopped).
Braking: gradient = (0 − 20) ÷ 5 = −4 m/s². A deceleration of 4 m/s².
Higher For the same car, find the total distance travelled.
Distance = area under the velocity–time graph. Split it into three shapes.
Triangle (speeding up): ½ × 8 × 20 = 80 m.
Rectangle (cruising): 10 × 20 = 200 m.
Triangle (braking): ½ × 5 × 20 = 50 m.
Total: 80 + 200 + 50 = 330 m. (Or as one trapezium: ½ × (23 + 10) × 20 = 330 m ✓)
Water is poured at a constant rate into a container that is wide at the bottom and narrow at the top. Describe the depth–time graph.
Bottom (wide): the water spreads out, so the depth rises slowly: a shallow gradient.
Top (narrow): the same volume each second makes a bigger rise in depth: a steeper gradient.
Answer: the graph starts shallow and gets steeper. If the width changes gradually (a cone) it's a smooth curve; if it changes suddenly (a wide cylinder with a narrow neck) it's two straight lines with a corner.
Higher A car's distance is d = 0.6t² metres after t seconds. Find its average speed between t = 2 and t = 6, and estimate its speed at t = 5 using the tangent shown through (2.5, 0) and (10, 45).
Average speed (chord): d(2) = 0.6 × 4 = 2.4 m; d(6) = 0.6 × 36 = 21.6 m. Gradient = (21.6 − 2.4) ÷ (6 − 2) = 19.2 ÷ 4 = 4.8 m/s.
Instantaneous speed (tangent): two points on the tangent are (2.5, 0) and (10, 45).
Gradient = (45 − 0) ÷ (10 − 2.5) = 45 ÷ 7.5 = 6 m/s at t = 5.
Interpret: at the instant t = 5 s the car is travelling at 6 m/s. The units are m/s because the gradient is metres per second.

Quick check

What you've learned

  • The gradient of a real-life graph is a rate of change, measured in y-units per x-unit (£ per mile, litres per minute, km/h). A straight line means a constant rate; a negative gradient means the quantity is decreasing. The y-intercept is usually the starting value.
  • Distance–time graphs: gradient = speed; horizontal = stationary; sloping down = returning. Convert minutes to hours for km/h.
  • Average speed = total distance ÷ total time, the gradient of the line from start to finish. It is not the mean of the speeds.
  • Velocity–time graphs: gradient = acceleration (m/s²); horizontal = constant speed; sloping down = decelerating. Higher: the area under the graph = distance travelled.
  • Filling containers at a constant rate: narrow parts give steep sections, wide parts give shallow sections; gradual changes in width give curves.
  • Higher: on a curve, the average rate between two points is the gradient of the chord; the instantaneous rate at a point is the gradient of the tangent there. Interpret both with units.
