Sampling

You want to know something about a huge group of people or things, but you can't check them all. So you check a few and use them to say something about everyone. This page is about doing that well: choosing a sample fairly, spotting when a sample will mislead you, and knowing how much to trust what it tells you.

GCSE subject content (Statistics): infer properties of populations or distributions from a sample, whilst knowing the limitations of sampling; apply statistics to describe a population

Before you start. You need percentages and fractions of amounts (fractions, decimals and percentages), the mean (averages and range) and the idea that more trials give a more reliable relative frequency (theoretical and experimental probability). The Higher sections use proportion (dividing in a ratio).

1 · Populations and samples

Suppose a head teacher wants to know how the 1200 pupils at her school get to school. The whole group she wants to know about is called the population. It doesn't have to be people: it could be every battery a factory made today, or every fish in a lake.

She could ask all 1200 pupils. Collecting data from every member of the population is called a census. Or she could ask, say, 60 of them and use their answers to estimate the picture for the whole school. The smaller group that you actually collect data from is a sample.

Why not always do a census? Three reasons come up again and again in exams:

  • Cost: asking or testing everyone costs money (staff, postage, equipment).
  • Time: a census of a big population takes a long time, and the answers may be out of date by the time you finish.
  • Destructive testing: sometimes the test destroys the thing being tested. To find how long a battery lasts you run it until it is flat. Test every battery and there are none left to sell.

A census makes sense when the population is small, or when you genuinely need information about every single member. Decide for each situation:

The price of sampling is uncertainty. A sample only tells you about the people in it. Everything you say about the population is an estimate, and it is only as good as the sample. The rest of this page is about making that estimate as trustworthy as possible.

2 · Random sampling

The fairest way to choose a sample is a random sample: every member of the population has the same chance of being chosen. No person gets picked because they are nearby, friendly or loud. In practice you do it like this:

  1. Get a list of the whole population (called a sampling frame, for example the school register).
  2. Number every member: 1, 2, 3, …, up to the size of the population.
  3. Use a random number generator (a calculator's RanInt button or a computer) to produce numbers in that range.
  4. Pick the members with those numbers. If a number comes up twice, ignore the repeat and generate another. Keep going until you have enough people.

Below is a Year 10 of 200 pupils, numbered 1 to 200. Some of them walk to school; you can't see which until they're in your sample. Press the generator one number at a time and watch what happens with repeats.

Exam phrase. "Describe how to take a random sample of 30 pupils from the 600 in Year 9" earns its marks for: number every pupil from 1 to 600; use a random number generator to pick 30 different numbers from 1 to 600 (ignoring repeats); survey the pupils with those numbers. "Pick names out of a hat" also counts if every name goes in. "Ask 30 pupils" does not: it says nothing about how they are chosen.

3 · Bias: when a sample stops representing the population

A sample is representative if it looks like a small copy of the population. A sample is biased if the way it was chosen makes some kinds of member more likely to be picked than others, so its results lean one way. Bias is a problem with the method, and a bigger sample does not cure it: ask 500 people at a gym about exercise and you get a very confident wrong answer.

Ways a sample becomes unrepresentative.

  • Convenience sample: asking whoever is easiest to reach (your friends, people at the gate, people in one place at one time). The place and time decide who is there.
  • Self-selecting (volunteer) sample: people choose whether to reply, and the ones who care most reply most.
  • Too small a sample: even a random sample of 4 can be wildly off by bad luck. That isn't bias, but it is unreliable (section 5).
  • Missing people from the list: a sampling frame that leaves some members out (only pupils with an email address, only people with a landline) can never pick them.

To improve a biased sample in an exam answer: take a random sample from the whole population, and make it larger.

4 · Bias in the question itself

Even a perfectly chosen sample gives biased results if the question pushes people towards an answer. A leading question suggests the "right" answer ("Don't you agree…?", "the excellent new…"). Response boxes cause trouble too: if they overlap (£0–£5 and £5–£10: where does £5 go?), leave gaps, have no box for some answers, or use vague words like "often" that mean different things to different people. Personal questions asked face to face get dishonest answers. Decide whether each question is fair or has a problem.

5 · Sample size and reliability

In a town, 40% of adults support a new skate park, but the pollsters don't know that: they want to estimate it. Each poll asks a random sample and reports the sample proportion: the fraction of the sample who said yes. For example, 9 yes out of 20 gives 9 ÷ 20 = 0.45 = 45%.

Every random sample gives a slightly different answer just by chance. Take lots of samples of size 5, 20 and 100 and see how spread out their answers are. Each dot is one poll; the dashed line is the true 40%.

The dots for samples of 100 huddle around the true value; the dots for samples of 5 are scattered all over. So a larger sample is more reliable: its estimate is likely to be closer to the truth, and repeating it gives similar answers. (To halve the spread you need about four times the sample size, which is why polls of 1000 people are common and polls of 1 000 000 are not.)

Two different things. Bias pushes every result the same wrong way, and a bigger sample does not fix it. Random variation scatters results around the truth, and a bigger sample does shrink it. Using a sample to estimate a population figure: if 18 out of a random sample of 50 pupils walk, estimate 18 ÷ 50 = 36% of all pupils walk. Out of 900 pupils, that's about 0.36 × 900 = 324.

6 · Stratified sampling Higher

Sometimes a population splits into groups that might answer differently: year groups, age bands, full-time and part-time staff. These groups are called strata (singular stratum). A pure random sample might, by chance, have too many Year 7s. A stratified sample makes sure each group appears in the same proportion as in the population, then picks randomly within each group.

Concrete numbers first. A school has 1000 pupils, 240 of them in Year 7. Year 7 is 240 ÷ 1000 = 24% of the school, so it should be 24% of the sample. For a sample of 50, that's 0.24 × 50 = 12 Year 7s. In one line:

group's share = group size ÷ population size × sample size

Try it

Exam traps. Don't split the sample equally between the groups: that is only right if the groups are the same size. Round each answer to a whole number (you can't survey 9.375 people), then check the group numbers add up to the sample size. Within each group the people are still chosen at random.

7 · Capture–recapture: counting fish you can't see Higher

How many fish are in a lake? You can't drain it to count them. The capture–recapture method uses a sample twice:

  1. Capture some fish, say M = 40. Mark each one (a harmless tag) and put them back.
  2. Wait for them to mix back in with the rest.
  3. Recapture: catch a second sample, say n = 50, and count how many of them, m, are marked.

The idea: if the second sample is a fair sample, the fraction of it that is marked should match the fraction of the whole lake that is marked. If 8 of the 50 are marked, then about 8 in 50 of all the fish are marked. We know there are 40 marked fish, so

m ÷ n ≈ M ÷ N  so  N ≈ M × n ÷ m

Here N ≈ 40 × 50 ÷ 8 = 250 fish. Try it on a lake where the true number is hidden.

Assumptions (exams often ask for one): the population doesn't change between the two catches (no births, deaths, fish arriving or leaving); the marks don't fall off; the marked fish mix fully back in; and marked fish are just as likely to be caught as unmarked ones. Because it rests on a sample, the answer is an estimate, and recapturing again gives a slightly different one.

Worked examples

Census or sample? Testing light bulbs

A factory makes 20 000 light bulbs a week. The manager wants to know the mean lifetime of the bulbs. Explain why she should use a sample.

What does the test involve? To find a bulb's lifetime you leave it on until it fails.
So the test is destructive. If every bulb were tested there would be none left to sell. (It would also take a long time and cost a lot.)
Answer: testing destroys the bulbs, so she should test a random sample of them and use the sample mean as an estimate of the mean lifetime of all the bulbs.
Describe how to take a random sample

Describe how to choose a random sample of 30 members from a gym with 750 members.

List and number: take the list of all 750 members and number them 1 to 750.
Generate: use a random number generator to produce whole numbers from 1 to 750.
Repeats: ignore any number that has already been picked, and keep going until there are 30 different numbers.
Select: the members with those 30 numbers form the sample. Each member had the same chance of being chosen.
Exam-style: criticise a sample

To find out how much exercise people in a town do, Sam asks the first 40 people leaving a leisure centre on a Saturday morning. Give two reasons why his sample may be biased, and say how to improve it.

Where: people leaving a leisure centre have just exercised, so they probably exercise more than the town as a whole. People who never exercise can't be picked.
When: Saturday morning leaves out people who work on Saturdays, and may favour some age groups.
How chosen: "the first 40" is a convenience sample: not everyone in the town had the same chance.
Improve: number everyone on a list of the whole town (for example, the electoral register), choose a random sample with a random number generator, and take a larger sample.
Using a sample to estimate a population

A random sample of 50 of the 900 pupils at a school found that 18 walk to school. Estimate how many pupils at the school walk.

Sample proportion: 18 ÷ 50 = 0.36 (that's 36%).
Assume the population has the same proportion: 0.36 × 900 = 324.
Answer: about 324 pupils walk. Say "about" or "estimate": a different random sample could give a slightly different answer.
Stratified sample with rounding Higher

A sports club has 125 juniors, 210 adults and 65 seniors. A stratified sample of 30 members is taken. How many of each should be in the sample?

Population size: 125 + 210 + 65 = 400.
Juniors: 125 ÷ 400 × 30 = 0.3125 × 30 = 9.375 → 9.
Adults: 210 ÷ 400 × 30 = 0.525 × 30 = 15.75 → 16.
Seniors: 65 ÷ 400 × 30 = 0.1625 × 30 = 4.875 → 5.
Check: 9 + 16 + 5 = 30 ✓. The members within each group are then chosen at random.
Capture–recapture Higher

Ecologists catch 40 newts in a pond, mark them and release them. A week later they catch 50 newts and find that 8 are marked. Estimate the number of newts in the pond, and state one assumption.

Fraction of the second sample that is marked: 8 ÷ 50 = 0.16.
Assume the same fraction of the whole pond is marked: 40 ÷ N = 0.16.
Solve: N = 40 ÷ 0.16 = 250. (Same as 40 × 50 ÷ 8 = 2000 ÷ 8 = 250.)
Answer: about 250 newts. Assumption: no newts were born, died, arrived or left during the week (also accepted: the marks don't come off; the marked newts mixed back in).

Quick check

What you've learned

  • The population is the whole group you want to know about. A census collects data from all of it; a sample collects data from part of it.
  • We sample to save cost and time, and because some tests are destructive. A census is sensible when the population is small or every member matters.
  • In a random sample every member has the same chance of being chosen: number every member, use a random number generator, ignore repeats.
  • A biased sample leans one way because of how it was chosen: convenience samples, volunteers, a sampling frame that leaves people out. Leading questions and badly designed response boxes bias the answers too.
  • Larger random samples are more reliable: their estimates are less spread out and usually closer to the true value. A bigger sample does not fix bias.
  • A sample proportion estimates a population proportion: proportion × population size estimates how many.
  • Higher A stratified sample takes group size ÷ population size × sample size from each group, rounded, chosen randomly within the group.
  • Higher Capture–recapture: N ≈ M × n ÷ m, assuming the population doesn't change, marks stay on and the fish mix fully.
