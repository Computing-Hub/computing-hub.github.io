Enlargement with Negative Scale Factors

An enlargement changes a shape's size but not its shape. A scale factor of 2 doubles it; a scale factor of ½ halves it. But what could a scale factor of −2 mean? The image appears on the other side of the centre, twice as big and upside down. This page builds up from the KS3 idea to negative scale factors, then works backwards to find the centre and scale factor from a picture.

GCSE subject content G7: identify, describe and construct congruent and similar shapes, including on coordinate axes, by considering rotation, reflection, translation and enlargement (including fractional and, at Higher, negative scale factors).

1 · Recap: enlargement from a centre

From the KS3 page Transformations: an enlargement needs a centre and a scale factor k. Every point of the image is k times as far from the centre as the matching point of the object, along the same straight line (a ray) through the centre. Count it as a vector: from the centre to a vertex is, say, 21; multiply by k = 3 to get 63, and go that far from the centre.

Drag the plum centre.

2 · Fractional scale factors

A scale factor between 0 and 1 makes the image smaller and pulls it towards the centre. It's still called an enlargement in maths. With k = ½ each vertex goes half as far from the centre; with k = ⅓, a third as far.

Trap: a scale factor of ½ does not mean "subtract 2" or "halve the coordinates". Halve the distances from the centre. Only when the centre is the origin (0, 0) do the coordinates simply halve.

3 · Negative scale factors Higher

A negative scale factor means: go the same distance as for the positive version, but in the opposite direction through the centre. The vector from the centre to each vertex is multiplied by a negative number, which reverses it. So the image ends up on the other side of the centre, and because every point is reversed, it's upside down (rotated 180°). The size still changes by the size of k: −2 doubles lengths, −½ halves them.

Drag the centre, and drag the object's corners.

Special case: k = −1 gives an image the same size, upside down, on the other side: it's exactly a rotation of 180° about the centre. And k = 0 would squash everything onto the centre, so it's never used.

4 · Constructing it step by step Higher

To draw an enlargement with a negative scale factor without guessing, do one vertex at a time with vectors. Step through for k = −2 and centre (1, 1).

5 · Finding the centre and scale factor Higher

Given an object and its image, work backwards:

  1. Centre: join each vertex to its matching image vertex with a straight line (A to A′, B to B′, …). All the lines cross at one point: the centre. For a negative scale factor the centre lies between the shapes.
  2. Scale factor: divide an image length by the matching object length. Make it negative if the image is on the opposite side of the centre (upside down).

Click the grid point that is the centre of enlargement, then type the scale factor (fractions like −1/2 are fine).

Describe it fully. "Enlargement, scale factor −2, centre (1, 1)." You need all three parts: the word enlargement, the scale factor and the centre as coordinates.

Worked examples

Enlarge the triangle A(1, 1), B(3, 1), C(1, 2) by scale factor 2, centre the origin.
With centre (0, 0), the vector from the centre to each point is just its coordinates; multiply by 2.
A′(2, 2), B′(6, 2), C′(2, 4). Check: AB = 2, A′B′ = 4 ✓.
Enlarge the triangle (4, 2), (6, 2), (4, 6) by scale factor ½, centre (0, 0).
Multiply each position by ½: (2, 1), (3, 1), (2, 3).
The image is half the size and closer to the centre.
Find the image of P(2, 3) under an enlargement with scale factor −2, centre (1, 1). Higher
Vector from the centre to P: (2 − 1, 3 − 1) = 12.
Multiply by −2: −2−4.
Go that far from the centre: (1 − 2, 1 − 4) = P′(−1, −3).
Describe the single transformation that is an enlargement with scale factor −1, centre (0, 0). Higher
Every point (x, y) goes to (−x, −y): same distance from the origin, opposite direction.
That is a rotation of 180° about (0, 0).
Triangle A(2, 1), B(4, 1), C(2, 2) maps to A′(−4, −2), B′(−8, −2), C′(−4, −4). Describe the transformation fully. Higher
Centre: the line through A(2, 1) and A′(−4, −2) and the line through B(4, 1) and B′(−8, −2) both pass through (0, 0).
Size: AB = 2, A′B′ = 4, so the lengths are × 2.
Sign: the image is on the opposite side of the centre and upside down, so k is negative.
Answer: enlargement, scale factor −2, centre (0, 0).
Find the image of P(6, 4) under an enlargement with scale factor −½, centre (2, 0). Higher
Vector from centre to P: (6 − 2, 4 − 0) = 44.
× −½: −2−2. From the centre: (2 − 2, 0 − 2) = P′(0, −2).

Quick check

What you've learned

  • An enlargement needs a centre and a scale factor k. Multiply the vector from the centre to each vertex by k.
  • 0 < k < 1: smaller image, closer to the centre (still called an enlargement).
  • Higher: k < 0 puts the image on the other side of the centre and turns it upside down; lengths are multiplied by the size of k. k = −1 is a rotation of 180°.
  • To find the centre, join matching vertices; the lines meet at the centre. Scale factor = image length ÷ object length, negative if the image is inverted on the other side.
  • Describe fully: enlargement, scale factor, centre.
