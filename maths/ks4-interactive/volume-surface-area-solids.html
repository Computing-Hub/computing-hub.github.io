Cylinders, Cones, Spheres and Pyramids

A tin of beans, an ice-cream cone, a football, the Great Pyramid. You know how to find the volume of a box and of a prism; this page extends that to every common solid, shows why a cone holds exactly a third of a cylinder, unrolls curved surfaces into flat shapes you can measure, and finishes with litres and composite solids.

GCSE subject content G16: know and apply formulae to calculate the volume of cuboids and other right prisms (including cylinders). G17: calculate surface area and volume of spheres, pyramids, cones and composite solids. N13: use standard units of measure, including volume and capacity.

1 · Cylinders and their nets

Recap from KS3 (Perimeter, area and volume and 3D shapes): a prism has the same cross-section all the way through, and its volume is area of cross-section × length. A cylinder is a prism with a circular cross-section, so

Volume of a cylinder = πr² × h = πr²h

For the surface area, unpeel the label from a tin. It comes off as a rectangle: one side is the height h, and the other wrapped exactly once round the circle, so it's the circumference, 2πr. Add the two circular ends:

Curved surface = 2πr × h = 2πrh
Total surface area = 2πr² + 2πrh

2 · Cones: slant height and curved surface

A cone has a circular base and comes to a point (the apex). Three lengths matter: the radius r, the vertical height h (apex straight down to the centre of the base) and the slant height l (apex down the side to the edge). They make a right-angled triangle, so by Pythagoras (KS3 Pythagoras):

l² = r² + h²

Cut a cone up the side and flatten it: the curved surface becomes a sector of a circle with radius l. Its arc wraps round the base, so the arc is 2πr. The full circle of radius l would have circumference 2πl and area πl², so the sector is the fraction 2πr ÷ 2πl = r/l of it:

Curved surface of a cone = r/l × πl² = πrl
Total surface area = πrl + πr²

Trap: the curved surface uses the slant height l; the volume uses the vertical height h. If you're given one, use Pythagoras to get the other.

3 · Why a cone is a third of a cylinder (and a pyramid a third of a prism)

Take a cone and a cylinder with the same base and the same height. Fill the cone with water and pour it in. It takes exactly three cones to fill the cylinder. The same is true for any pyramid and the prism with the same base and height.

Cone: V = ⅓πr²h Pyramid: V = ⅓ × base area × h

(Why a third? One proof: a cube can be cut into exactly three identical square-based pyramids, each with its apex at the same corner. Each is ⅓ of the cube. Squash or stretch the cube and the pieces stay equal, so ⅓ works for any pyramid.)

4 · Spheres and hemispheres

A sphere is a perfectly round ball; a hemisphere is half of one. Archimedes found the formulas by fitting a sphere snugly inside a cylinder (radius r, height 2r). The sphere's volume is exactly ⅔ of the cylinder, and its surface area equals the cylinder's curved surface. A cone in the same cylinder is ⅓, so cone + sphere = cylinder.

Sphere: V = ⁴⁄₃πr³ S = 4πr²
Hemisphere: V = ⅔πr³ curved S = 2πr², total S = 2πr² + πr² = 3πr²

The exam usually gives you the sphere and cone formulas, but you must know how to use them. Don't forget the flat circular face of a hemisphere when you find its total surface area.

5 · Frustums Higher

A frustum is what's left when you slice the top off a cone with a cut parallel to the base: a lampshade or a bucket. Volume of frustum = big cone − small cone. The small cone is similar to the big one, so its radius scales with its height: r = R × h/H (see Similar shapes).

6 · Composite solids

A composite solid is made from simpler solids stuck together. Volume: add the volumes of the parts. Surface area: add only the surfaces you can see from outside; where two parts are joined, that face is hidden and doesn't count.

7 · Units: cm³, m³ and litres

Capacity is how much a container holds, measured in litres (l) and millilitres (ml). A cube 10 cm × 10 cm × 10 cm holds exactly 1 litre, so 1 litre = 1000 cm³ and 1 ml = 1 cm³. A cube 1 m on each side is 100 × 100 × 100 = 1 000 000 cm³, which is 1000 litres. Volume units jump by the cube of the length factor: 1 m = 100 cm, so 1 m³ = 100³ cm³.

Trap: 1 m³ is not 100 cm³. The cube has 100 cm on every edge: 100 × 100 × 100 = 1 000 000 cm³.

8 · Practice

New question each time. Answers to 3 s.f., or as a number in front of π when asked (type 20 for 20π).

Worked examples

A cylinder has radius 4 cm and height 10 cm. Find its volume and total surface area in terms of π.
Volume = πr²h = π × 4² × 10 = π × 16 × 10 = 160π cm³ (= 503 cm³ to 3 s.f.).
Two ends = 2 × π × 4² = 32π. Curved = 2π × 4 × 10 = 80π.
Total = 32π + 80π = 112π cm² (= 352 cm² to 3 s.f.).
A cone has radius 6 cm and vertical height 8 cm. Find its slant height, total surface area and volume.
Slant height: l² = 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100, so l = 10 cm.
Curved surface = πrl = π × 6 × 10 = 60π. Base = π × 6² = 36π. Total = 96π cm² = 301.59… = 302 cm² (3 s.f.).
Volume = ⅓πr²h = ⅓ × π × 36 × 8 = 96π cm³ = 302 cm³ (3 s.f.). (Same number as the area here by coincidence: different units!)
A ball has radius 3 cm. Find its volume and surface area to 3 s.f.
Volume = ⁴⁄₃ × π × 3³ = ⁴⁄₃ × 27π = 36π = 113.097… = 113 cm³.
Surface area = 4 × π × 3² = 36π = 113 cm² (3 s.f.). (r = 3 is the radius where they match numerically.)
A solid hemisphere has radius 5 cm. Find its volume and total surface area to 3 s.f.
Volume = ½ × ⁴⁄₃πr³ = ⅔ × π × 125 = 250π/3 = 261.79… = 262 cm³.
Curved surface = ½ × 4πr² = 2π × 25 = 50π. Flat face = π × 25 = 25π.
Total = 75π = 235.61… = 236 cm². (50π alone forgets the flat circle.)
A square-based pyramid has base 6 cm by 6 cm and vertical height 10 cm. Find its volume.
Base area = 6 × 6 = 36 cm².
Volume = ⅓ × 36 × 10 = 120 cm³. (Without the ⅓ you'd get the cuboid, 360 cm³.)
A cone with base radius 6 cm and height 12 cm has its top 6 cm cut off. Find the volume of the frustum in terms of π. Higher
Small cone: height 6 is half of 12, so (similar shapes) its radius is half of 6: r = 3 cm.
Big cone = ⅓ × π × 6² × 12 = 144π. Small cone = ⅓ × π × 3² × 6 = 18π.
Frustum = 144π − 18π = 126π cm³ (= 396 cm³ to 3 s.f.).
Composite: an ice cream is a cone (radius 3 cm, height 10 cm) topped with a hemisphere of radius 3 cm. Find its volume, and give it in litres.
Cone = ⅓ × π × 3² × 10 = 30π. Hemisphere = ⅔ × π × 3³ = 18π.
Total = 48π = 150.79… = 151 cm³ (3 s.f.).
Litres: ÷ 1000 → 0.150796… = 0.151 litres (151 ml).

Quick check

What you've learned

  • Cylinder: V = πr²h; curved surface 2πrh (unrolls to a 2πr × h rectangle); total 2πr² + 2πrh.
  • Cone: slant height l from l² = r² + h²; curved surface πrl (unrolls to a sector); V = ⅓πr²h, a third of the cylinder.
  • Pyramid: V = ⅓ × base area × height.
  • Sphere: V = ⁴⁄₃πr³, S = 4πr². Hemisphere: V = ⅔πr³, total S = 3πr² (don't forget the flat face).
  • Higher: frustum = big cone − small cone, using similar shapes to find the small cone.
  • Composite solids: add volumes; for surface area count only the outside faces.
  • 1 litre = 1000 cm³, 1 ml = 1 cm³, 1 m³ = 1 000 000 cm³ = 1000 litres.
