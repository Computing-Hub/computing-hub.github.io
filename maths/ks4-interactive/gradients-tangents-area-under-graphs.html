Gradients, Tangents and Area Under Graphs

The gradient of a graph tells you how fast something is changing; the area under it tells you how much has built up. For straight lines both are easy. For curves we estimate them: the gradient with a tangent, the area with trapezia. These two ideas are the doorway to calculus.

GCSE subject content: interpret the gradient of a straight-line graph as a rate of change; Higher: calculate or estimate gradients of graphs and areas under graphs (including non-linear graphs) and interpret the results, e.g. distance–time and velocity–time graphs; average and instantaneous rates of change (gradients of chords and tangents)

Recap. The gradient of a straight line is rise ÷ run (change in y ÷ change in x); see Linear graphs. You'll also need the area of a triangle (½ × base × height) and of a trapezium (½(a + b)h). For lots of real-life examples (distance–time, velocity–time, kinematics), see the companion page Rates of change graphs; this page concentrates on the methods.

1 · The gradient of a line is a rate of change

When the axes measure real quantities, the gradient has units: "units of y per unit of x". A rate of change is how much one quantity changes for each unit of another, like metres per second, litres per minute or pounds per kilogram. On a straight line the rate is the same everywhere. Drag the two plum points along the line.

2 · Curves: average rate of change and chords Higher

A ball rolls down a slope. The distance it has travelled is d = t² metres after t seconds. The graph curves upwards: the ball keeps speeding up, so there is no single gradient. A chord is a straight line joining two points on a curve. Its gradient is the average rate of change between those points: here, the average speed.

Move the two points closer together. The chord's gradient settles down towards a single value: the speed at one instant. That limit is the gradient of the tangent.

3 · Tangents and instantaneous rate of change Higher

A tangent to a curve is a straight line that just touches the curve at one point, going in the same direction as the curve there. The gradient of the curve at a point is defined as the gradient of the tangent at that point. In context it is the instantaneous rate of change, for example the speed at exactly t = 3 seconds.

In an exam you draw the tangent with a ruler, by eye. Below, P is the point. Turn your plum ruler until it touches the curve at P without cutting across it. Then shrink the orange chord PQ to see what the true gradient is.

Drawing a good tangent. Use a sharp pencil and a ruler. Make the line long. Near the point, the curve should be on the same side of your line on both sides of P (or the line is crossing it, which means it's a chord, not a tangent). To read the gradient, pick two points on your tangent (not on the curve) that are far apart, and work out rise ÷ run with a big triangle: it's more accurate than a small one.

4 · Area under a straight-line graph Higher

On a velocity–time graph, velocity (speed in a direction) is on the y-axis and time on the x-axis. Area means height × width, and here that is (metres per second) × (seconds) = metres. So the area under a velocity–time graph is the distance travelled. Split the area into triangles, rectangles and trapezia. Drag the velocity at each time.

The gradient of the same graph is (m/s) ÷ s = m/s², the acceleration. A negative gradient is deceleration. Don't mix them up: gradient gives acceleration; area gives distance.

5 · Area under a curve: the trapezium method Higher

When the graph is curved, there is no simple shape for the area. Split it into vertical strips of equal width. Replace the curve across each strip with a straight line, so each strip becomes a trapezium (a four-sided shape with two parallel sides). Add up the trapezia. More strips give a better estimate.

Over or under? If the curve bends upwards (like a U, gradient increasing), each straight top sits above the curve, so the trapezia over-estimate. If it bends downwards (like an ∩), they under-estimate. Exam questions often ask you to say which, and why.

6 · Putting it together: a journey Higher

A car pulls away and later slows down: v = 4t − ½t² m/s for 0 ≤ t ≤ 8. Slide the time. The plum tangent's gradient is the acceleration at that instant; the shaded area up to that time is the distance so far, estimated with half-second trapezia.

Worked examples

A tank holds 20 litres at the start and 80 litres after 12 minutes, filling steadily. Find the rate of flow.
Two points on the line: (0, 20) and (12, 80).
Gradient: (80 − 20) ÷ (12 − 0) = 60 ÷ 12 = 5.
With units: y is litres, x is minutes, so the rate is 5 litres per minute.
For d = t², find the average speed between t = 1 and t = 3 Higher
Points: t = 1, d = 1; t = 3, d = 9.
Chord gradient: (9 − 1) ÷ (3 − 1) = 8 ÷ 2 = 4 m/s.
Meaning: the ball covers 8 m in 2 s. It is slower than 4 m/s at t = 1 and faster at t = 3.
Estimate the gradient of y = x² at x = 3 Higher
Draw the tangent at (3, 9) with a ruler so it touches without crossing.
Pick two points on the tangent, far apart: e.g. (1, −3) and (5, 21).
Gradient: (21 − (−3)) ÷ (5 − 1) = 24 ÷ 4 = 6. A hand-drawn tangent gives an estimate, so answers like 5.8 or 6.2 are usually accepted.
A car accelerates from rest to 12 m/s in 4 s, travels at 12 m/s for 6 s, then stops steadily in 3 s. Find the acceleration at the start and the total distance. Higher
Acceleration: gradient of the first part = 12 ÷ 4 = 3 m/s².
Area, piece by piece: triangle ½ × 4 × 12 = 24; rectangle 6 × 12 = 72; triangle ½ × 3 × 12 = 18.
Total distance: 24 + 72 + 18 = 114 m. (Or one trapezium: ½(6 + 13) × 12 = 114 ✓)
Use 4 strips of equal width to estimate the area under y = x² + 1 from x = 0 to x = 4. Is it an over- or under-estimate? Higher
Strip width: (4 − 0) ÷ 4 = 1. Heights: x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 give y = 1, 2, 5, 10, 17.
Trapezia: ½(1 + 2) × 1 = 1.5; ½(2 + 5) × 1 = 3.5; ½(5 + 10) × 1 = 7.5; ½(10 + 17) × 1 = 13.5.
Total: 1.5 + 3.5 + 7.5 + 13.5 = 26 square units.
Over-estimate: the curve bends upwards, so each straight top lies above the curve. (The true area is 25⅓.)
The tangent to a distance–time graph at t = 5 s passes through (2, 0) and (8, 19.2). What does its gradient represent? Higher
Gradient: 19.2 ÷ 6 = 3.2.
Interpret: metres ÷ seconds, so it is the speed at t = 5 s: 3.2 m/s (an instantaneous rate, not an average).

What you've learned

  • The gradient of a straight-line graph is a rate of change, with units "y-units per x-unit".
  • Higher The gradient of a chord is the average rate of change between two points; the gradient of a tangent is the instantaneous rate of change at one point.
  • Higher Estimate a tangent's gradient by drawing it carefully and using a large rise ÷ run triangle on the tangent.
  • Higher The area under a velocity–time graph is the distance travelled; its gradient is the acceleration.
  • Higher Estimate the area under a curve by splitting it into equal-width trapezia and adding them. Curving upwards → over-estimate; curving downwards → under-estimate.
