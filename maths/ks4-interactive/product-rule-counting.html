The Product Rule for Counting

How many different outfits can you make from 3 tops and 2 pairs of trousers? How many 4-digit PINs are there? How many ways can 8 runners finish first, second and third? You could list them all, but lists get long fast. This page starts by listing carefully, spots the multiplication hiding in every list, and turns it into a rule that counts thousands of possibilities in one line.

DfE GCSE subject content (Number): apply systematic listing strategies; Higher tier: including use of the product rule for counting

What you need from KS3

  • Listing outcomes in a sample space, and using a two-way table or tree diagram to show every combination. Recap on sample spaces and Venn diagrams.
  • Probability of equally likely outcomes: number of ways it can happen ÷ total number of outcomes.
  • Digits are 0 to 9 (ten of them); even numbers end in 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8; a 3-digit number can't start with 0.
  • Powers: 104 = 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10 000.

1 · Listing systematically

An outcome is one possible result, here one complete outfit. To list every outcome without missing or repeating any, be systematic: fix the first choice, run through every option for the second, then move the first choice on. A tree diagram does this automatically: each branch is one choice. Press "list the next outfit" and watch the list fill in order.

Listing mistakes: jumping about ("red with jeans, blue with chinos, red with chinos…") makes it easy to miss one or count one twice. Keep one choice fixed while the other runs through all its options, exactly like the tree.

2 · Spotting the multiplication: the product rule Higher

Look at the tree again. Each of the 3 tops sprouts the same 2 branches, so there are 3 groups of 2: 3 × 2 = 6 outfits. Change the sliders above and the count is always tops × trousers. That is the product rule for counting ("product" means the answer to a multiplication):

m ways × n ways = m × n ways

If one choice can be made in m ways and, for each of those, a second choice can be made in n ways, then together there are m × n ways. It works for any number of stages: just keep multiplying. A restaurant offers starters, mains and desserts; a meal is one of each.

Multiply, don't add. 2 starters, 3 mains and 2 desserts give 2 × 3 × 2 = 12 meals, not 2 + 3 + 2 = 7. You'd only add if you were choosing one dish from the whole menu (a starter or a main or a dessert).

3 · Codes and PINs: repetition allowed Higher

A 4-digit PIN has four positions (slots). Each slot can be any of the 10 digits, and digits can repeat (1111 is allowed). So there are 10 choices for every slot:

10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 104 = 10 000

That matches common sense: the PINs are exactly 0000, 0001, … up to 9999, which is ten thousand codes. In general, with k symbols and L slots and repeats allowed, there are kL codes.

4 · No repeats: 10 × 9 × 8 … Higher

Now suppose things can't be reused: a runner can't finish both first and second, and some codes ban repeated digits. Each time you fill a slot, one option gets used up, so the next slot has one fewer choice. Tap the runners in the order they finish and watch the number of choices shrink.

SituationCountWhy
4-digit PIN, repeats allowed10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10 000Every slot has all 10 digits.
4-digit PIN, no repeats10 × 9 × 8 × 7 = 5040Each slot has one fewer digit left.
Gold, silver, bronze from 8 runners8 × 7 × 6 = 336A runner can only finish in one place.

Read the question for repetition. "Digits can be repeated" → the same number every slot. "No digit is used more than once" or choosing different people → count down by 1 each slot. Mixing these up is the most common product rule mistake.

5 · Restrictions: fill the fussy slot first Higher

Real questions add restrictions: a 3-digit number can't start with 0; an even number must end in 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8. The strategy: fill the most restricted slot first, then the others. Below, the orange slot is the one filled first. The grid shows every number from 100 to 999; the ones that fit are shaded, so you can check that the product rule really does count them.

Why the order matters: for odd 3-digit numbers with all digits different, if you fill the hundreds first (9 ways) and then the units, the number of odd digits left depends on what you picked: if the hundreds digit was odd, only 4 odd digits remain; if it was even, 5 do. Filling the units first (5 odd digits) avoids that problem: then hundreds has 8 (not 0, not the units digit) and tens has 8 (anything except the two used). 5 × 8 × 8 = 320.

6 · Counting and probability

The product rule tells you the size of a sample space, the list of all equally likely outcomes. A coin and a dice give 2 × 6 = 12 outcomes, which is exactly the number of cells in the two-way table. Tap a cell: its probability is 1 over the total. (See also the sample spaces page.)

The same idea works for huge sample spaces you'd never draw. A thief guessing a 4-digit PIN at random has a probability of 110 000 of getting it first time. A random 3-digit number is even with probability 450900 = 12.

Worked examples

List all the outfits from tops {red, blue} and trousers {jeans, chinos, shorts}
Fix red: red + jeans, red + chinos, red + shorts.
Fix blue: blue + jeans, blue + chinos, blue + shorts.
Total: 6 outfits. Check: 2 tops × 3 trousers = 6 ✓
4 tops, 3 pairs of trousers and 2 pairs of shoes: how many outfits? Higher
Three independent choices, one from each group.
Product rule: 4 × 3 × 2 = 24 outfits. (Not 4 + 3 + 2 = 9.)
8 runners race. In how many ways can gold, silver and bronze be awarded? Higher
Gold: any of 8 runners. Silver: any of the 7 left. Bronze: any of the 6 left.
Product rule: 8 × 7 × 6 = 336 ways.
Trap: 8 × 8 × 8 = 512 would let one runner win two medals.
How many 4-digit whole numbers are there? Higher
Restricted slot first: the first digit can't be 0, so 9 choices (1–9).
Other slots: 10 choices each (repeats allowed).
Total: 9 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 9000. Check: the numbers 1000 to 9999 are 9999 − 1000 + 1 = 9000 ✓
How many odd 3-digit numbers have all their digits different? Higher
Most restricted slot first, the units: must be odd: 1, 3, 5, 7 or 9, so 5 choices.
Next most restricted, the hundreds: not 0 and not the units digit: 10 − 2 = 8 choices.
Tens: any digit except the two already used: 8 choices.
Total: 5 × 8 × 8 = 320.
A number plate has 2 letters followed by 3 digits, repeats allowed. How many plates are possible? Higher
Letters: 26 × 26 = 676. Digits: 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000.
Total: 676 × 1000 = 676 000 plates.
Exam-style: a club of 20 members chooses a captain and a vice-captain. Show that there are 380 ways. Higher
Captain: 20 choices. Vice-captain: anyone except the captain, so 19 choices.
Product rule: 20 × 19 = 380 ✓. The order matters here (captain ≠ vice-captain), which is why it isn't halved.

Quick check

What you've learned

  • List outcomes systematically: fix one choice and run through every option for the next. A tree diagram does this for you.
  • The product rule (Higher): if one thing can be done in m ways and another in n ways, together there are m × n ways. Keep multiplying for more stages.
  • Repetition allowed: every slot has the same number of choices, so k symbols in L slots gives kL.
  • No repetition: one fewer choice each time, like 10 × 9 × 8.
  • Restrictions: fill the most restricted slot first (no leading 0, must be even, must be odd), then count what's left for the others.
  • The product rule gives the size of a sample space, so P(one particular outcome) = 1 ÷ (total number of outcomes).
