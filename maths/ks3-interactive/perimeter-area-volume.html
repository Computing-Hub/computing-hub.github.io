Perimeter, Area and Volume

How much fence goes round a garden? How much carpet covers a floor? How much water fills a fish tank? Those are three different questions, and they need three different measurements: perimeter (a length), area (squares that fit) and volume (cubes that fit). This page builds each one from scratch, so you can see where every formula comes from instead of just memorising it.

National curriculum KS3: derive and apply formulae to calculate and solve problems involving perimeter and area of triangles, parallelograms and trapezia, volume of cuboids (including cubes) and other prisms; calculate and solve problems involving perimeters of 2-D shapes and composite shapes; use standard units of measure

1 · Perimeter: the distance around

Imagine an ant walking all the way round the edge of a shape, starting at a corner and stopping when it gets back to where it began. The total distance it walks is the perimeter. The word comes from Greek: peri means "around" and metron means "measure". So perimeter is simply "the measure around".

Because perimeter is a distance, it is measured in ordinary length units: millimetres (mm), centimetres (cm), metres (m) or kilometres (km). A fence, a picture frame, the white line round a football pitch and the crust round a slice of toast are all perimeters.

A rectangle is a four-sided shape whose corners are all right angles (square corners, 90°). Its opposite sides are equal, so it has two long sides (the length) and two short sides (the width). Press the button to walk the ant round one side at a time. Each orange dot is 1 cm along the way.

Your turn

Notice the pattern. The ant always walks length, width, length, width. So for any rectangle:

perimeter = length + width + length + width = 2 × (length + width)

Common mistakes with perimeter

  • Adding only the two sides you can see labelled. A 5 cm by 3 cm rectangle has perimeter 16 cm, not 8 cm. Opposite sides are equal, so both labels count twice.
  • Mixing units. If one side is 2 m and another is 50 cm, change them to the same unit first (200 cm and 50 cm) before adding.
  • Mixing it up with area. Perimeter is the edge (a length, in cm). Area is the inside (in cm²). Next section.

2 · Area: how many squares fit

Area measures how much flat surface a shape covers: the inside, not the edge. Think of tiling a bathroom floor or covering a table with sticky notes. To measure area we need a "unit" of surface, just as we used 1 cm as a unit of length. The obvious choice is a little square that is 1 cm long and 1 cm wide. It is called one square centimetre, written 1 cm². The small ² is a reminder that two lengths (1 cm across and 1 cm up) were used to make it.

So "the area is 15 cm²" means exactly this: 15 of those little squares fit inside, with no gaps and no overlaps. Drag the purple corner (or use the sliders) to make any rectangle and count the squares.

You never actually need to count one by one. The squares come in neat rows. A 5 cm by 3 cm rectangle has 3 rows with 5 squares in each, so there are 3 × 5 = 15 squares. That is why:

area of a rectangle = length × width

A square is a rectangle with all four sides equal, so a square with side 4 cm has area 4 × 4 = 16 cm². (This is where the word "squared" for 4² comes from.)

Perimeter and area are different things. A 6 cm × 2 cm rectangle and a 4 cm × 4 cm square both have perimeter 16 cm, but their areas are 12 cm² and 16 cm². Try both in the grid above. Same fence, different amount of field inside.

Units: area always gets a "squared" unit: mm², cm², m², km². Writing "15 cm" for an area is a mistake: 15 cm is a length.

3 · Triangles: half a rectangle

A triangle doesn't fill whole squares neatly, so counting is awkward. Instead we use a trick: draw a rectangle around it.

Two new words first. The base is the side the triangle sits on. The perpendicular height (or just "height") is how tall the triangle is, measured straight up from the base at a right angle to it. "Perpendicular" means "meeting at a right angle", like the corner of a page. The height is the dashed orange line: it is not one of the sloping sides.

The dashed line cuts the rectangle into two smaller rectangles. Each small rectangle is cut in half by a sloping side of the triangle: one half is inside the triangle (teal) and the other half is outside (plum). The numbers show each piece's area, and they match in pairs. So the triangle is exactly half of the rectangle. Drag the purple top corner left and right and watch: the area never changes.

Drag the purple top corner along the top edge.

Your turn

area of a triangle = ½ × base × height

"½ ×" means "half of", so you can also multiply base × height and then divide by 2. Both give the same answer.

Common mistakes with triangles

  • Forgetting to halve. base × height is the rectangle round the triangle. The triangle is only half of that.
  • Using the sloping side as the height. The height must be perpendicular to the base. A sloping side is always longer than the true height, so it gives an answer that is too big.

4 · Parallelograms: cut and slide

Two lines are parallel if they run in the same direction and never meet, like railway tracks. A parallelogram is a four-sided shape whose opposite sides are parallel. It looks like a rectangle that has been pushed over sideways.

Here is the trick. Cut off the triangle at one end along the dashed height line, then slide it across to the other end. It fits perfectly, and the parallelogram becomes a rectangle. Nothing was added or thrown away, so the area hasn't changed. Move the slider (or press Play).

5 · Trapezia: two make a parallelogram

A trapezium (plural trapezia) is a four-sided shape with exactly one pair of parallel sides. We call the two parallel sides a and b, and the perpendicular distance between them is the height h. Think of the side view of a bucket, or a slice of a pyramid.

The trick this time: take a second, identical trapezium, turn it upside down and push it against the first. Together they make a parallelogram! Its base is a + b (one of each side joined end to end) and its height is still h. Move the slider to rotate the copy into place.

Your turn

The parallelogram has area (a + b) × h, and it is made of two identical trapezia, so one trapezium is half of it:

area of a trapezium = ½ × (a + b) × h

In words: add the parallel sides, multiply by the height, then halve. The brackets matter: you add a and b first (BIDMAS).

Common mistakes with trapezia: forgetting the ½ (that gives the area of the whole parallelogram, twice too big); multiplying a × b × h instead of adding a and b first; using a sloping side instead of the perpendicular height.

6 · Composite shapes: split it up

A composite shape is one made by joining simpler shapes together. The L-shape below is a good example. There is no special formula for it, and you don't need one: split it into rectangles, find each area, then add. Or think of it as a big rectangle with a piece cut out, and subtract.

Choose a method. Every method gives the same answer, which is a great way to check your work.

Your turn

Missing sides. Exam questions often leave some sides unlabelled. Find them by subtracting: if the whole bottom is 10 cm and the top is 6 cm, the step across must be 10 − 6 = 4 cm.

Perimeter of a composite shape: go all the way round and add every outside edge, including the little inside steps. Do not include the cut lines you drew to split the shape: they are inside, so the ant never walks along them.

Don't overlap. When you split a shape, each square must belong to exactly one piece. If your pieces overlap, you count some squares twice.

7 · Volume: how many cubes fit

Now step up from flat shapes to solid ones. Volume measures how much space a 3-D object takes up, or how much it holds: how much water fills a tank, how much air is in a room. Just as area counts squares, volume counts cubes. A cube that is 1 cm long, 1 cm wide and 1 cm tall is called one cubic centimetre, written 1 cm³. It is about the size of a sugar cube or a dice. The small ³ shows that three lengths made it.

A cuboid is a box shape: six rectangular faces, all corners right angles. Think of a cereal box or a brick. Fill one with 1 cm cubes, one layer at a time.

Your turn

The bottom layer is just a rectangle of cubes: length × width of them. The height tells you how many identical layers are stacked up. So:

volume of a cuboid = length × width × height

A cube with side 3 cm holds 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 cm³. (That is why 3³ is called "3 cubed".)

Useful check: count the lengths you multiplied. One length → cm. Two lengths → cm². Three lengths → cm³. If you multiplied three lengths and wrote cm², something has gone wrong.

Same units first: a box 2 m by 50 cm by 30 cm must become 200 cm × 50 cm × 30 cm = 300 000 cm³. Multiplying 2 × 50 × 30 = 3000 mixes metres with centimetres and is wrong.

Capacity: 1000 cm³ = 1 litre, and 1 cm³ = 1 millilitre (ml).

8 · Prisms: cross-section × length

A prism is a solid that has the same shape all the way through, like a stick of rock, a Toblerone bar or a loaf of sliced bread. That shape is called the cross-section: it is the face you would see if you sliced straight through the prism. A cuboid is a prism whose cross-section is a rectangle.

Think of a prism as a pile of identical 1 cm thick slices. Each slice has volume equal to the area of the cross-section (a slice of area 6 cm² and thickness 1 cm holds 6 cubes). If the prism is 8 cm long there are 8 slices. So multiply!

Your turn

volume of a prism = area of cross-section × length

This is the same idea as the cuboid. For a cuboid, the cross-section is a rectangle with area length × width, and its "length" through the prism is the height. So length × width × height is just a special case.

Two-step method: (1) find the area of the cross-section using the right 2-D formula; (2) multiply by the length. Write the units: cm² after step 1, cm³ after step 2.

Common mistake: multiplying every number you can see. For a triangular prism with base 6, height 4 and length 10, 6 × 4 × 10 = 240 is wrong: the cross-section is a triangle, so it's ½ × 6 × 4 = 12 cm², and the volume is 12 × 10 = 120 cm³.

Worked examples

A rectangular garden is 8 m long and 5 m wide. Find its perimeter and its area.
Perimeter (fence round the edge): add all four sides. 8 + 5 + 8 + 5 = 26 m.
Check with the shortcut: 2 × (8 + 5) = 2 × 13 = 26 m ✓
Area (grass inside): 5 rows of 8 one-metre squares. 8 × 5 = 40 m².
Units: the perimeter is a length (m); the area counts squares (m²).
A triangle has base 10 cm and perpendicular height 7 cm. Find its area.
Formula: area = ½ × base × height.
Substitute: ½ × 10 × 7.
Work it out: 10 × 7 = 70 (the rectangle round the triangle). Half of 70 is 35.
Answer: 35 cm².
A parallelogram has base 9 cm, sloping sides of 6 cm and perpendicular height 5 cm. Find its area.
Pick the right numbers: area = base × perpendicular height. The 6 cm sloping side is not needed: it's there to catch you out.
Work it out: 9 × 5 = 45 cm².
Sense check: 9 × 6 = 54 would be too big, because the shape is "squashed" compared with a 9 by 6 rectangle.
A trapezium has parallel sides 6 cm and 10 cm, and height 4 cm. Find its area.
Formula: area = ½ × (a + b) × h.
Brackets first: 6 + 10 = 16.
Multiply by the height: 16 × 4 = 64 (that's the parallelogram made of two trapezia).
Halve: 64 ÷ 2 = 32 cm².
An L-shape is a 10 cm by 8 cm rectangle with a 4 cm by 3 cm corner cut out. Find its area and perimeter.
Method 1, subtract: whole rectangle 10 × 8 = 80 cm². Missing corner 4 × 3 = 12 cm². Area = 80 − 12 = 68 cm².
Method 2, split (to check): left piece is 10 − 4 = 6 cm wide and 8 cm tall: 6 × 8 = 48 cm². Right piece is 4 cm wide and 8 − 3 = 5 cm tall: 4 × 5 = 20 cm². Total 48 + 20 = 68 cm² ✓
Perimeter: the six outside edges are 10 (bottom), 5 (right), 4 (step across), 3 (step up), 6 (top) and 8 (left). 10 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 6 + 8 = 36 cm.
A fish tank is a cuboid 50 cm long, 30 cm wide and 40 cm tall. How much water does it hold?
One layer: 50 × 30 = 1500 cubes, each 1 cm³.
Number of layers: 40, so volume = 1500 × 40 = 60 000 cm³.
In litres: 1000 cm³ = 1 litre, so 60 000 ÷ 1000 = 60 litres.
A tent is a triangular prism. The triangle has base 6 m and height 4 m, and the tent is 15 m long. Find its volume.
Step 1, cross-section: ½ × 6 × 4 = ½ × 24 = 12 m².
Step 2, times the length: 12 × 15 = 180 m³.
Check the units: three lengths multiplied (6, 4 and 15), so m³ ✓. Note 6 × 4 × 15 = 360 is exactly double: that's what forgetting the ½ gives.

Quick check

What you've learned

  • Perimeter is the distance all the way round the edge. Add every side. It is a length, in mm, cm, m or km. For a rectangle: 2 × (length + width).
  • Area is how many 1-unit squares fit inside, in mm², cm², m². Rectangle: length × width (rows × squares in each row).
  • Triangle = ½ × base × perpendicular height, because it is exactly half of the rectangle round it.
  • Parallelogram = base × perpendicular height, because cutting off one end and sliding it across makes a rectangle.
  • Trapezium = ½ × (a + b) × h, because two copies make a parallelogram with base a + b.
  • Composite shapes: split into simple shapes and add, or take a big shape and subtract the missing part. Find missing sides by subtracting.
  • Volume is how many 1-unit cubes fit inside, in cm³ or m³. Cuboid: length × width × height (one layer × number of layers).
  • Prism: area of cross-section × length. Find the 2-D area first, then multiply.
  • Always use the perpendicular height, convert to the same units before calculating, and check the unit: one length → cm, two → cm², three → cm³.
