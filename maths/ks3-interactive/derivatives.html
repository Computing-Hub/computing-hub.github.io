Derivatives

The mathematics of how fast things change. A derivative tells you the gradient of a curve at a single point, and that one idea powers speedometers, weather models, drug dosing, and the way AI learns.

Builds on GCSE Higher: gradients of straight lines, drawing tangents to estimate gradient, rate of change

Why do we need them?

You already know how to work out speed: distance ÷ time. If a car covers 120 km in 2 hours, its speed is 60 km/h. But that is its average speed. The speedometer doesn't show an average. It shows the speed right now, at this instant.

Here's the problem. "Right now" lasts zero seconds, and the car travels zero metres in zero seconds. Zero ÷ zero is meaningless. Yet the speedometer clearly shows a number. How?

The answer is the derivative (la derivada). It's the mathematical tool for finding the rate of change at an instant, invented by Newton and Leibniz, separately, in the 1660s–70s, to solve exactly this puzzle. Getting there takes five small steps.

Step 1 · Gradient of a straight line

Gradient (la pendiente) means steepness: how far up you go for each step across. Pick any two points on the line and divide rise by run.

m = riserun = y₂ − y₁x₂ − x₁

Through (1, 3) and (4, 9): rise 6, run 3, gradient 2. Try (2, 5) and (10, 21): rise 16, run 8, gradient 2 again. On a straight line it doesn't matter which two points you choose. The line is equally steep everywhere, and that single number describes all of it.

Step 2 · The problem with a curve

Now take y = x². Pick (1, 1) and (3, 9): gradient 4. Pick (1, 1) and (2, 4): gradient 3. Different answer. A curve has no single steepness: gentle near the bottom, steep further out. So "what is the gradient of y = x²?" has no answer.

The question has to change to: what is the gradient at one particular point?

Step 3 · Secante and tangente

Two words you'll remember from school in Spain. A line that cuts the curve at two points is a secant (recta secante, from the Latin secare, to cut). A line that just touches the curve at one point, matching its direction there, is the tangent (recta tangente, from tangere, to touch). The gradient of the curve at a point is the gradient of the tangent there.

Notice: where the curve is flat (a peak or a trough), the gradient is zero. Where it climbs steeply, the gradient is large and positive. Where it falls, the gradient is negative.

But there's a catch. Rise over run needs two points. A tangent touches at only one. One point gives rise 0 and run 0, and 0 ÷ 0 is meaningless. So how do we ever calculate the gradient of a tangent?

Step 4 · The trick: squeeze a secant

We cheat. Start with a secant, which we can measure: the point we care about, plus a second point a small distance h further along. Then make h smaller and smaller. As the second point slides toward the first, the secant swings round until it lies on the tangent.

Here it is in numbers, for y = x² at x = 1:

Step 5 · What a limit is

"Heading to 2 without ever reaching it" has a name: a limit (límite). A limit answers the question where is this going? rather than what is it when you arrive? Here's the cleanest example of why that matters.

Take g(x) = (x² − 4) ÷ (x − 2). Put x = 2 in and you get 0 ÷ 0: undefined, a hole in the graph. But look at what happens on either side of the hole:

Numerator x² − 4
Denominator x − 2
g(x)
Distance from 4

From both sides g(x) is heading to 4. We write limx→2 g(x) = 4, said "the limit as x tends to 2 of g of x is 4". The arrow means approaches, never equals. The function has no value at 2, yet its limit there is perfectly clear.

Why 4? Because x² − 4 = (x − 2)(x + 2), so for every x except 2 the function is simply x + 2. The troublesome factor cancels. That cancelling move is the engine of all of calculus.

Three things that trip people up. (1) You never reach the value: h = 0.001 is a step, 0 is the destination. (2) A limit can exist where the function doesn't, like the hole above. (3) If the two sides disagree there is no limit: 1/x near 0 shoots to +∞ from the right and −∞ from the left, so 1/x has no gradient at 0.

Step 6 · The derivative, defined properly

Put Steps 4 and 5 together. The secant gradient is rise over run with the second point at x + h:

f′(x) = limh→0 f(x + h) − f(x)h

In Spain this fraction is called the cociente incremental and the whole statement is la derivada es el límite del cociente incremental. Same idea, same formula.

Do it by hand for y = x². Rise = (x + h)² − x² = 2xh + h². Divide by h: 2x + h. Now h → 0 and you are left with 2x. At x = 1 that gives 2 (matching the table), at x = 3 it gives 6, at x = 0 it gives 0, the flat bottom of the bowl. The derivative is a new function that hands you the gradient at any x you like.

Reading the notation

Two ways of writing the derivative survive, from its two inventors.

NotationSaidWhere it comes from
f′(x), y′"f dash of x", "y prime"Newton's school (via Lagrange). Compact; the one Spanish textbooks use most.
dy/dx"dee y by dee x"Leibniz. The d means "a tiny difference" (diferencial): a tiny change in y divided by a tiny change in x. It is rise over run with both pieces shrunk toward zero.
∫ f(x) dx"the integral of f of x, dee x"Also Leibniz. The long S is a stretched letter S for summa, a sum. It means "add up all the tiny pieces f(x)·dx". It's the reverse operation to d/dx and belongs to the second page.

Leibniz's symbols won because they behave like fractions. Writing speed as ds/dt reminds you it is a tiny distance over a tiny time, and the long S reminds you that integration is just adding those tiny pieces back up: ∫ (ds/dt) dt = s, the dt's cancel.

Where derivatives are actually used

Every field that deals with change uses them. Some you'll recognise from other subjects.

Physics: motion

Differentiate displacement to get velocity. Differentiate velocity to get acceleration. If a ball's height is s = 20t − 5t², its velocity is v = 20 − 10t, zero at t = 2 s (the top of the flight).

Economics: marginal cost

If making x items costs C(x), then C′(x) is the cost of making one more. Firms expand production until marginal cost equals the price they can sell for.

Medicine: dosing

Drug concentration in the blood rises then falls. The derivative tells doctors how fast it's being cleared, which sets how often you take the next dose.

Computing: machine learning

Neural networks learn by gradient descent: compute the derivative of the error with respect to each weight, then nudge every weight downhill. Billions of derivatives per second.

Engineering: optimisation

Minimum material, maximum strength, least drag. Any "best" value sits where the derivative is zero, because that's where the quantity stops rising and starts falling.

Biology & epidemiology

Population growth rate, the spread of an infection, the "R number": all describe how fast a count is changing. The classic growth curve's derivative is what makes exponentials so dangerous.

Worked example: the biggest box

You have a 30 cm × 30 cm square of card. Cut equal squares from each corner and fold up the sides to make an open box. What cut size gives the largest volume? Try the slider first, then see how the derivative finds the answer exactly.

Hint
Solve it with the derivative
1. Write the volume. Base is (30 − 2x) by (30 − 2x), height x. So V = x(30 − 2x)² = 900x − 120x² + 4x³.
2. Differentiate. V′ = 900 − 240x + 12x².
3. Set it to zero. 12x² − 240x + 900 = 0 → divide by 12: x² − 20x + 75 = 0 → (x − 5)(x − 15) = 0.
4. Pick the sensible root. x = 15 would leave no base at all. So x = 5 cm, giving V = 5 × 20² = 2000 cm³.
5. Check it's a maximum. V′ is positive just before 5 and negative just after: the volume rises then falls. Maximum confirmed.

Worked example: a falling object

Height of a stone: h = 45 − 5t²
Question. A stone is dropped from a 45 m cliff. Its height after t seconds is h = 45 − 5t². How fast is it falling when it hits the ground?
When does it land? h = 0 → 5t² = 45 → t = 3 s.
Velocity is the derivative. dh/dt = −10t.
At t = 3: dh/dt = −30. It hits the ground at 30 m/s (the minus sign just means downward). That's about 108 km/h.
Bonus. Differentiate again: d²h/dt² = −10. Acceleration is constant at −10 m/s², which is gravity. Two derivatives took you from "where is it" to "what force acts on it".

Quick check

What you've learned

A straight line has one gradient; a curve has a different gradient at every point, given by its tangent. You can't measure a tangent from one point, so you measure a secant and take the limit as the two points squeeze together. That limit of the cociente incremental is the derivative: an instantaneous rate of change. The power rule does the work: bring the power down, reduce it by one. Setting a derivative to zero finds maximums and minimums, which is the basis of every optimisation problem from box design to training AI.
