Mutually Exclusive Outcomes

When you spin a spinner, exactly one thing happens: it lands on one colour, never two at once. That simple fact gives one of the most useful rules in probability: if you list every possible outcome, and no two of them can happen together, their probabilities add up to exactly 1. On this page you'll learn the words for this, see why it works, and use it to find probabilities nobody has told you.

National curriculum KS3: apply the property that the probabilities of an exhaustive set of outcomes sum to one; apply the property that the probabilities of an exhaustive set of mutually exclusive events sum to one

1 · Can both happen at the same time?

Some things simply can't happen together. A traffic light can't be on red and on green at the same moment. You can't be in London and in Paris at the same time. When you flip a coin once it lands on heads or tails, never both.

First, two words. An outcome is one possible result, like "the dice shows 4". An event is an outcome or a group of outcomes you are interested in, like "the dice shows an even number" (that's the outcomes 2, 4 and 6). If you need a reminder of how to work out a probability, such as P(even) = 3 ÷ 6, see the probability scale page.

Two events are mutually exclusive if they cannot happen at the same time. "Mutually" means "each other", and "exclude" means "keep out": each event keeps the other one out.

  • Mutually exclusive: rolling a 2 and rolling a 5 on one dice. One roll shows one number.
  • Not mutually exclusive: on a spinner where sector 2 is red, landing on "red" and landing on "an even number" can happen together: sector 2 is both. We say the events overlap.

The test: try to find one single outcome that is in both events. If you can find even one, the events are not mutually exclusive. If there is no such outcome, they are.

2 · Exhaustive: nothing left out

A list of events is exhaustive if it covers every possible outcome, so that one of them is certain to happen. ("Exhaustive" is like "exhausting" all the possibilities: you've used them all up.)

On one dice the outcomes are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. The events "even" and "odd" are exhaustive: every number is one or the other. But "less than 3" and "more than 3" are not exhaustive, because 3 has been left out.

The most useful lists are both: every outcome is in exactly one event. Think of sorting socks into drawers: every sock goes in a drawer (exhaustive) and no sock is in two drawers at once (mutually exclusive).

3 · Sharing out the 1

A probability of 1 means certain. If your list of outcomes is exhaustive, one of them is certain to happen, so together they have probability 1. If the outcomes are also mutually exclusive, none of them overlap, so that 1 is shared out between them with nothing counted twice, like slices of one cake that together make the whole cake.

Concrete example first: a fair dice has six outcomes, each with probability 1/6. Add them: 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 = 6/6 = 1.

Now drag the white handles on the spinner (or on the bar underneath) to change the sizes of the sectors. However you share it out, the four probabilities always add to exactly 1, because the bar is always exactly 1 long and is always completely filled.

P(A) + P(B) + P(C) + … = 1

…as long as the outcomes A, B, C, … are mutually exclusive and exhaustive. The same is true of results from experiments: the relative frequencies of all the outcomes add up to 1 too (see theoretical and experimental probability).

4 · The chance it doesn't happen: P(not A)

A weather app says there is a 30% chance of rain. What is the chance of no rain? You probably already know: 70%. As probabilities that is 0.3 and 0.7, and notice that 0.3 + 0.7 = 1.

For any event A, the event "not A" means "A does not happen". A and not A can't both happen (mutually exclusive) and one of them must happen (exhaustive). So they share out the 1:

P(A) + P(not A) = 1, so P(not A) = 1 − P(A)

5 · Finding a missing probability in a table

Exam questions often give a table of probabilities for every outcome with one missing. Because the outcomes are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, the full list must add up to 1. So:

  1. Add up the probabilities you know.
  2. Take that total away from 1. What's left is the missing probability.
  3. Check: all of them together should now make exactly 1.

Example: P(red) = 0.2, P(blue) = 0.5, P(green) = ?. Known total 0.2 + 0.5 = 0.7, so P(green) = 1 − 0.7 = 0.3. Check: 0.2 + 0.5 + 0.3 = 1 ✓

With an unknown x. Sometimes the missing probabilities are written with a letter, such as 0.2, 0.35, 2x and x. Write "everything adds to 1" as an equation and solve it: 0.2 + 0.35 + 2x + x = 1, so 0.55 + 3x = 1, so 3x = 0.45, so x = 0.15. Notice 2x + x is 3x, so you divide by 3 at the end.

6 · The OR rule: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)

A bag has 10 counters: 3 red, 2 blue and 5 green. What is the probability of taking a counter that is red or blue? Count them: 3 + 2 = 5 of the 10 counters, so 5/10. And look: P(red) + P(blue) = 3/10 + 2/10 = 5/10. Adding works, because no counter is both red and blue.

If A and B are mutually exclusive:
P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)

But if the events overlap, adding counts the overlapping outcomes twice and gives an answer that is too big. Choose two events on the spinner below and compare "add the probabilities" with "count the sectors".

Only add when the events can't happen together. When they overlap, list or count the outcomes instead. Diagrams that show overlapping events clearly, called Venn diagrams, are on the sample spaces and Venn diagrams page.

7 · Common mistakes and how to spot them

  • Probabilities adding to more than 1. A full list of mutually exclusive outcomes must add to exactly 1. If yours make 1.2, something is wrong.
  • Using 100 instead of 1. Certain is 1. "100 − 0.3" gives 99.7, which is impossible. (100 is only right if everything is in percentages: 100% − 30% = 70%.)
  • Subtracting from the wrong value (or the wrong way round). The missing probability is 1 minus the total, not one probability minus another, and not 0.7 − 1.
  • Adding overlapping events. P(A) + P(B) only gives P(A or B) when A and B are mutually exclusive.
  • Stopping too soon with x. If you reach 3x = 0.45, you still have to divide by 3.

Worked examples

A spinner can land on red, blue or green. P(red) = 0.3 and P(blue) = 0.45. Find P(green).
Can I use "add to 1"? The spinner lands on exactly one colour (mutually exclusive) and these are the only colours (exhaustive). Yes.
Add the ones I know: 0.3 + 0.45 = 0.75.
Take away from 1: P(green) = 1 − 0.75 = 0.25.
Check: 0.3 + 0.45 + 0.25 = 1 ✓ (0.25 is the same as 1/4.)
The probability a train is late is 0.08. Find the probability it is not late. Then: P(Sam wins) = 3/7; find P(Sam does not win).
Late and not late are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, so P(not late) = 1 − P(late).
P(not late) = 1 − 0.08 = 0.92. Check: 0.08 + 0.92 = 1 ✓
With fractions, write 1 as a fraction with the same denominator: 1 = 7/7.
P(Sam does not win) = 7/7 − 3/7 = 4/7. Check: 3/7 + 4/7 = 7/7 = 1 ✓
The table shows probabilities for a bag of counters: red 0.2, blue 0.35, green 2x, yellow x. Find x and P(green).
Everything adds to 1: 0.2 + 0.35 + 2x + x = 1.
Collect like terms: 0.2 + 0.35 = 0.55 and 2x + x = 3x, so 0.55 + 3x = 1.
Subtract 0.55 from both sides: 3x = 1 − 0.55 = 0.45.
Divide both sides by 3: x = 0.45 ÷ 3 = 0.15.
P(green) = 2x = 2 × 0.15 = 0.3.
Check: 0.2 + 0.35 + 0.3 + 0.15 = 1 ✓
Outcomes A, B and C are mutually exclusive and exhaustive. P(A) = 1/4 and P(B) = 1/3. Find P(C).
Common denominator: 12 works for both 4 and 3. 1/4 = 3/12 and 1/3 = 4/12.
Add: 3/12 + 4/12 = 7/12.
Take away from 1 = 12/12: P(C) = 12/12 − 7/12 = 5/12.
Check: 3/12 + 4/12 + 5/12 = 12/12 = 1 ✓
A counter is taken from a bag. P(red) = 0.15 and P(blue) = 0.4. Find P(red or blue) and P(neither red nor blue).
Mutually exclusive? One counter can't be red and blue, so yes: use the OR rule.
P(red or blue) = 0.15 + 0.4 = 0.55.
"Neither" is "not (red or blue)": P = 1 − 0.55 = 0.45.
A fair dice is rolled. Find P(even or more than 4).
List each event: even = 2, 4, 6 (3/6); more than 4 = 5, 6 (2/6).
Overlap? 6 is in both, so they are not mutually exclusive. Adding 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6 would count the 6 twice: wrong.
Count instead: the outcomes that are even or more than 4 are 2, 4, 5, 6: four outcomes.
P(even or more than 4) = 4/6 = 2/3.

Quick check

What you've learned

  • An outcome is one possible result; an event is an outcome or a group of outcomes.
  • Events are mutually exclusive if they cannot happen at the same time. To test, look for one outcome in both: if there is one, they overlap.
  • A set of outcomes is exhaustive if it includes every possibility, so one of them must happen.
  • The probabilities of a set of mutually exclusive, exhaustive outcomes add up to exactly 1.
  • P(not A) = 1 − P(A).
  • To find a missing probability: add the known ones and take the total away from 1. With x, write "total = 1" as an equation, collect the x terms and solve.
  • For mutually exclusive events, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). If events overlap, adding counts the overlap twice, so count the outcomes instead.
  • Sense checks: no probability is below 0 or above 1, use 1 not 100, and a full list must total exactly 1.
