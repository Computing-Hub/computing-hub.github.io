Integration

The mathematics of adding up. Integration finds the total when something is changing: the distance covered when speed varies, the charge delivered when current fluctuates, the area under any curve you can write down.

Builds on GCSE Higher: area under a velocity–time graph, estimating area with trapezia

This is exactly what you did at GCSE with trapezia, only taken to its logical end: infinitely many slices, each infinitely thin. The sum stops being an estimate and becomes exact. That limit is the integral.

Why do we need it?

A car's speedometer reads 50 km/h, then 70, then 30 as the traffic changes. How far has it gone after an hour? If the speed were constant it'd be easy: distance = speed × time. But speed keeps changing, so no single multiplication works.

On a velocity–time graph, that multiplication is the area of a rectangle: speed (height) × time (width). When speed varies, the region under the graph isn't a rectangle any more. But the distance is still the area under the curve. Integration is how you find that area.

The same logic applies to anything that accumulates: water flowing into a tank at a varying rate, money earning compound interest, a battery charging at falling current, a drug building up in the bloodstream.

So ∫ab f(x) dx reads, almost literally, as "add up the areas of all the infinitely thin rectangles of height f(x) and width dx between a and b". It is the Riemann-rectangle slider above with n pushed to infinity. The old finite sum Σ f(xᵢ) Δx becomes ∫ f(x) dx: Σ turns into ∫, and the finite step Δx turns into the infinitesimal dx.

Without limits, ∫ f(x) dx is an indefinite integral (integral indefinida): the answer is a function, the family of all primitivas (antiderivatives). With limits it is a definite integral (integral definida): the answer is a single number, an area.

Integration undoes differentiation

On the derivatives page you went from distance to speed by differentiating. Integration is the reverse journey. This connection, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, is the single most useful fact in the subject: you don't need rectangles at all if you can spot a function whose derivative is the one you have.

Differentiatex³ → 3x²
⇄
Integrate3x² → x³ + C

Where does the + C come from? The derivative of x³ is 3x². But so is the derivative of x³ + 5, or x³ − 100: constants vanish when you differentiate. Going backwards you can't tell which constant was there, so you write C to stand for "some constant". It's called the constant of integration.

Definite integrals: a number, not a function

When you want the actual area between two x-values, say from a to b, you write limits on the integral sign and the + C cancels out. The recipe: find a primitiva F, plug in the top limit, plug in the bottom limit, subtract.

∫ab f(x) dx = [F(x)]ab = F(b) − F(a)

In Spain this is taught as the Regla de Barrow (after Isaac Barrow, Newton's teacher); English textbooks fold it into the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. The square-bracket notation [F(x)]ab is just shorthand for "evaluate at b, evaluate at a, subtract".

Watch out for the sign. Area below the x-axis comes out negative. That's useful (a negative velocity means travelling backwards, so the displacement should go down), but if you want the actual size of a region you integrate the parts above and below separately and add their magnitudes.

Where integration is actually used

Physics: distance from speed

Integrate velocity to get displacement. Integrate acceleration to get velocity. A rocket's onboard computer does this continuously from its accelerometers to know where it is.

Electronics: charge

Current is charge per second, so charge = ∫ I dt. A phone's battery gauge integrates the current drawn to estimate what's left (called "coulomb counting").

Economics: total from marginal

If marginal cost tells you the cost of one more unit, integrating it gives the total cost of making x units. The reverse of the derivatives example.

Statistics: probability

For a continuous quantity like height, the probability of landing in a range is the area under the bell curve between those values. Every normal-distribution table was computed by integration.

Engineering: volumes and centres of mass

Spin a curve round an axis and integrate to get the volume of a vase, a turbine blade or a wine glass. Integrate again to find where it balances.

Medicine: drug exposure

The "area under the curve" of blood concentration against time (literally called AUC) measures total drug exposure. It decides whether a generic drug is equivalent to the original.

Worked example: how far did the train go?

A train leaves a station. Its speed in m/s after t seconds is v = 12t − t² for the first 12 seconds (it accelerates, then slows as it approaches a signal). How far has it travelled when it stops at t = 12?

Solve it exactly
1. Distance is the integral of speed. s = ∫₀¹² (12t − t²) dt.
2. Integrate term by term. 12t → 6t². t² → t³/3. So F(t) = 6t² − t³/3.
3. Plug in the limits. F(12) = 6(144) − 1728/3 = 864 − 576 = 288. F(0) = 0.
4. Subtract. Distance = 288 m. The 6-trapezium estimate in the panel above gets to about 284 m: close, but integration is exact.

Worked example: filling a tank

Water flows in at a rate of r = 10 − 2t litres per minute
Question. A tank starts empty. Water enters at r = 10 − 2t litres per minute until the tap closes at t = 5. How much water is in the tank?
Rate in → total in by integrating. V = ∫₀⁵ (10 − 2t) dt = [10t − t²]₀⁵.
Evaluate. (50 − 25) − (0 − 0) = 25 litres.
Sanity check. The graph of r is a straight line from 10 down to 0 over 5 minutes: a triangle with area ½ × 5 × 10 = 25. Integration agrees with the area formula, as it must.

What you've learned

Integration adds up infinitely many infinitely thin slices to find a total, which is the area under a curve. It reverses differentiation: add one to the power and divide by it, remembering + C. A definite integral with limits gives a number: F(b) − F(a). Whenever you know a rate and want the total, from speed to distance, current to charge, or marginal to total cost, integration is the tool.

Together with derivatives, this is calculus: one operation measures change, the other accumulates it, and each undoes the other.
