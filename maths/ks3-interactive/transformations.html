Transformations

Slide a book across a desk, look in a mirror, turn a key, zoom in on a photo. Each of these takes a shape and puts a copy of it somewhere else. In maths these moves are called transformations, and there are four you need: translation (slide), reflection (flip), rotation (turn) and enlargement (zoom). This page builds each one on a grid, one step at a time.

National curriculum KS3: identify properties of, and describe the results of, translations, rotations and reflections applied to given figures; construct similar shapes by enlargement, with and without coordinate grids

1 · Object and image

A transformation is a rule that moves a shape, or changes its size, to make a new shape. The shape you start with is called the object. The shape you end up with is called the image (like the image you see in a mirror or on a screen).

The corners of a shape are called vertices (one corner is a vertex). We label the object's vertices with capital letters A, B, C. The matching vertices of the image get the same letter with a small dash: A′, B′, C′, said "A dash" (or "A prime"). A′ is always the point that A moved to. Matching points like A and A′ are called corresponding points.

Pick a move below, then drag the slider to watch the object travel to its image. Throughout this page the object is teal, the image is orange, and the thing that controls the move (an arrow, a mirror, a centre point) is purple.

Notice that in a slide, flip and turn the shape stays exactly the same size. Only the zoom changes the size. We will come back to this in Section 6.

2 · Translation: sliding

A translation slides a shape without turning it, flipping it or changing its size. Think of pushing a chess piece across the board: every part of it moves the same distance in the same direction.

To say exactly how far it slides, we use a column vector. It is two numbers stacked in a tall bracket, like 3−2.

  • The top number is how far across: positive means right, negative means left.
  • The bottom number is how far up or down: positive means up, negative means down.

So 3−2 means "3 right, 2 down". It is the same order as coordinates: across first, then up. To find an image point, add the top number to the x-coordinate and the bottom number to the y-coordinate. For example A(−1, −1) translated by 3−2 goes to A′(−1 + 3, −1 − 2) = A′(2, −3).

The purple arrows show A moving across first and then up or down. The grey arrows show B and C. Every arrow is the same length and points the same way: that is what makes it a translation.

Common mistakes with translations

  • Swapping the numbers. Top is across (x), bottom is up/down (y). Never the other way round.
  • Writing it like a coordinate. (3, −2) is a place. A column vector is a movement, so write it as a column.
  • Counting the gap between the shapes. Count from one vertex to its own image, for example from A to A′, not from the edge of the object to the nearest edge of the image.
  • Going the wrong way. Always go from the object to the image (A to A′), not backwards.

Practice: describe the translation

The teal triangle has been translated to the orange one. Pick a vertex, say A, and count squares from A to A′: across first, then up or down. Type the column vector.

3 · Reflection: mirrors

A reflection flips a shape over a mirror line (also called the line of reflection), exactly like your reflection in a mirror. Two facts make every reflection work:

  • Each image point is the same distance from the mirror as its object point, but on the other side.
  • The line joining a point to its image (A to A′) crosses the mirror at a right angle. We say it is perpendicular to the mirror. So you always measure straight towards the mirror, never at a slant.

Mirror lines on a grid are named by their equation, a rule that every point on the line obeys:

  • x = 2 is a vertical line (up and down). Every point on it has x-coordinate 2, like (2, 0), (2, 5), (2, −3). The y-axis is x = 0.
  • y = −1 is a horizontal line (side to side). Every point on it has y-coordinate −1. The x-axis is y = 0.
  • y = x is the diagonal going uphill through (1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 3).
  • y = −x is the diagonal going downhill through (1, −1), (2, −2), (−3, 3).

Choose a mirror line and watch. The grey dashed lines join each vertex to its image; the purple dot on each is its midpoint, which always lands exactly on the mirror.

Two more things to spot. The image faces the other way: going round the object A → B → C is anticlockwise, but A′ → B′ → C′ is clockwise. And any point that sits on the mirror does not move at all (try x = 2 above: A and C are on the mirror, so A′ = A and C′ = C).

Common mistakes with reflections

  • Mixing up x = and y =. x = 3 is vertical (it crosses the x-axis at 3). y = 3 is horizontal. Plot two points on the line if unsure.
  • Diagonal mirrors. Measure at right angles to the mirror, which on a grid means corner to corner across the squares. Shortcuts: reflecting in y = x swaps the coordinates, (x, y) → (y, x). Reflecting in y = −x swaps them and changes both signs, (x, y) → (−y, −x).
  • Sliding instead of flipping. The image must be back to front. If it faces the same way, you have drawn a translation.

Practice: find the mirror line

Look at a vertex and its image. If they are level (same y), the mirror is vertical, x = …. If one is straight above the other (same x), the mirror is horizontal, y = …. The mirror sits exactly halfway between them. Otherwise, try a diagonal.

4 · Rotation: turning

A rotation turns a shape around a fixed point, like the hands of a clock turning around the middle, or a door swinging on its hinge. The fixed point is called the centre of rotation. It is the only point that does not move. To describe a rotation you need three things:

  • the angle: how far it turns. A quarter turn is 90°, a half turn is 180°.
  • the direction: clockwise (the way clock hands go) or anticlockwise (the opposite way). A half turn of 180° lands in the same place either way, so it needs no direction.
  • the centre: the point it turns around, given as coordinates.

The tracing paper trick. In class you will often be given tracing paper. Trace the object and mark the centre. Put your pencil point on the centre to pin it down, turn the paper by the angle, and the traced shape now sits where the image goes. The slider below does this for you. Tap or drag on the grid to move the purple centre.

Each vertex stays the same distance from the centre as it turns, so it moves along a circle. The grey lines from the centre to A and to A′ are equal in length, and the angle between them is the angle of the rotation.

Common mistakes with rotations

  • Forgetting the centre. "Rotate 90° clockwise" is not enough: the same turn about a different centre lands somewhere else. Move the centre above and watch the image jump.
  • Turning about a corner of the shape when the question gives a different centre.
  • Direction mix-ups. 90° clockwise is the same as 270° anticlockwise. Check with tracing paper.

Practice: describe the rotation

First the angle: has the shape turned a quarter (its sides now point at right angles to before) or a half (upside down)? Then the direction. Then find the centre: the point that is the same distance from A as from A′, and from B as from B′. Testing guesses with tracing paper works well.

5 · Enlargement: zooming

An enlargement changes the size of a shape but keeps its shape, like zooming in on a photo or a projector throwing a picture onto a wall. Two things describe it:

  • the scale factor: the number every length is multiplied by. Scale factor 2 makes every side twice as long; scale factor 3 makes it three times as long.
  • the centre of enlargement: the point the zoom happens from. Think of it as the bulb of the projector.

The method: from the centre, count how far it is to a vertex (across and up), multiply both numbers by the scale factor, and count that far from the centre again. That gives the image vertex. The purple dashed lines are called rays: straight lines from the centre through each object vertex, and the image vertex is on the same ray. Tap or drag on the grid to move the centre.

Fractional scale factors. A scale factor between 0 and 1, such as ½, makes the shape smaller: every distance from the centre is halved. Maths still calls it an enlargement, even though the image shrinks.

Extension: negative scale factors. A negative scale factor sends each point through the centre and out the other side, so the image is upside down on the opposite side of the centre. Scale factor −1 is the same as a 180° rotation about the centre.

Common mistakes with enlargements

  • Counting from the edge of the shape instead of the centre. Every distance is measured from the centre of enlargement. If the centre is 2 squares from A, then with scale factor 3, A′ is 6 squares from the centre, not 6 squares from A.
  • Adding instead of multiplying. Scale factor 2 doubles a side of 3 to 6. It does not add 2 to make 5.
  • Forgetting the centre when describing it. The same scale factor from a different centre puts the image somewhere else.

Practice: describe the enlargement

Scale factor: compare a pair of matching sides, image length ÷ object length. Centre: draw a straight line through A and A′, another through B and B′, and extend them. Where they cross is the centre.

6 · Same shape, same size?

Two shapes are congruent if they are exactly the same shape and size: you could cut one out and place it perfectly on top of the other (turning it over if needed). Two shapes are similar if they are the same shape but possibly a different size: all the angles match and every length has been multiplied by the same number.

7 · Describing a transformation fully

Exam questions often say "describe fully the single transformation". That means: name the transformation in one word, and give every piece of information needed to repeat it exactly. Missing one piece loses marks.

Practice: which description is full and correct?

Any of the four transformations could appear. Choose the description that is complete and correct. Wrong choices tell you what went wrong.

Worked examples

Translate triangle A(1, 2), B(4, 2), C(1, 4) by −5−3
Read the vector. Top −5: move 5 left, so subtract 5 from every x. Bottom −3: move 3 down, so subtract 3 from every y.
A(1, 2) → (1 − 5, 2 − 3) = A′(−4, −1).
B(4, 2) → (4 − 5, 2 − 3) = B′(−1, −1).
C(1, 4) → (1 − 5, 4 − 3) = C′(−4, 1).
Check: AB was 3 long and horizontal; A′B′ goes from x = −4 to x = −1, also 3 long and horizontal. Same size, same way up ✓
Reflect triangle A(3, 1), B(5, 1), C(5, 4) in the line x = 2
Find the mirror. x = 2 is a vertical line through (2, 0). The y-coordinates will not change; only the x-coordinates flip across the line.
A(3, 1) is 1 square right of the mirror, so A′ is 1 square left of it: x = 2 − 1 = 1. A′(1, 1).
B(5, 1) is 3 squares right, so B′ is 3 squares left: x = 2 − 3 = −1. B′(−1, 1).
C(5, 4) is also 3 squares right: C′(−1, 4).
Check: the midpoint of A and A′ is (2, 1), and of B and B′ is (2, 1), both on x = 2 ✓ The image faces the other way ✓
Rotate triangle A(3, 1), B(5, 1), C(3, 3) by 90° anticlockwise about (1, 0)
Work from the centre, not the origin. From the centre (1, 0), A(3, 1) is 2 right and 1 up.
Turn that movement a quarter turn anticlockwise. "Right" becomes "up" and "up" becomes "left". So 2 right, 1 up becomes 2 up, 1 left. From (1, 0): A′(0, 2).
B(5, 1) is 4 right, 1 up from the centre → 4 up, 1 left → B′(0, 4).
C(3, 3) is 2 right, 3 up → 2 up, 3 left → C′(−2, 2).
Check with tracing paper: AB pointed right (east); after a quarter turn anticlockwise A′B′ should point up (north). A′(0, 2) to B′(0, 4) points straight up ✓
Enlarge triangle A(1, 1), B(2, 1), C(1, 2) by scale factor 3, centre (−1, 0)
From the centre to A: (−1, 0) to (1, 1) is 2 right, 1 up. Multiply by 3: 6 right, 3 up. From the centre: (−1 + 6, 0 + 3) = A′(5, 3).
To B(2, 1): 3 right, 1 up → × 3 → 9 right, 3 up → B′(8, 3).
To C(1, 2): 2 right, 2 up → × 3 → 6 right, 6 up → C′(5, 6).
Check the lengths: AB = 1, A′B′ = 8 − 5 = 3 ✓ AC = 1, A′C′ = 6 − 3 = 3 ✓ Every length is × 3. (A common mistake is to count 3 times the distance from A instead of from the centre.)
Describe fully the single transformation that maps A(3, 1), B(7, 1), C(3, 5) onto A′(1, 1), B′(3, 1), C′(1, 3)
Which transformation? The image is smaller but the same shape, so it is an enlargement.
Scale factor: AB = 7 − 3 = 4, A′B′ = 3 − 1 = 2. Image ÷ object = 2 ÷ 4 = ½.
Centre: the line through A(3, 1) and A′(1, 1) is the horizontal line y = 1. The line through C(3, 5) and C′(1, 3) goes 2 left and 2 down each step, so continuing from C′(1, 3): (−1, 1). Both lines pass through (−1, 1).
Check: centre to B is (−1, 1) to (7, 1), 8 right; half of that is 4 right, giving (3, 1) = B′ ✓
Answer: Enlargement, scale factor ½, centre (−1, 1).

Quick check

What you've learned

  • A transformation maps an object onto an image; vertex A maps to A′, B to B′ and so on.
  • Translation slides every point the same way. Describe it with a column vector: top = across (+ right, − left), bottom = up/down (+ up, − down).
  • Reflection flips the shape in a mirror line. Each point and its image are the same perpendicular distance from the mirror. x = a is vertical, y = b is horizontal, y = x and y = −x are the diagonals.
  • Rotation turns the shape about a centre. Describe it with angle, direction (clockwise or anticlockwise, not needed for 180°) and centre. Tracing paper helps.
  • Enlargement multiplies every distance from the centre by the scale factor. Use rays from the centre. A scale factor between 0 and 1 makes the shape smaller; a negative one puts it upside down on the other side of the centre.
  • Translations, reflections and rotations give congruent images (same shape and size). Enlargements give similar images (same shape and angles, different size).
  • To describe fully, name one transformation and give all of its information: vector; mirror line; angle, direction and centre; or scale factor and centre.
