Constructions with Ruler and Compasses

With just a pencil, a ruler and a pair of compasses you can draw a perfect right angle, cut any line exactly in half, split any angle into two equal parts and build a triangle to exact sizes — no guessing and no measuring of angles. Each construction is a tiny chain of reasoning about equal distances, and on this page you will see why every one of them works.

National curriculum KS3: derive and use the standard ruler and compass constructions (perpendicular bisector of a line segment, constructing a perpendicular to a given line from/at a given point, bisecting a given angle); recognise and use the perpendicular distance from a point to a line as the shortest distance to the line

1 · What a pair of compasses does

A pair of compasses (often just called "compasses") is a tool with two legs joined at a hinge. One leg ends in a sharp metal point that you press into the paper; the other holds a pencil. The gap between the point and the pencil is called the width (or the radius). When you twist the compasses, the point stays still, so the pencil is always the same distance from it.

That is the whole secret: every point the pencil draws is exactly the same distance from the centre. A full turn draws a circle. A short twist draws an arc, which is just a piece of a circle. Try it: drag the pencil (P) round. Untick "lock the width" if you want to open or close the compasses.

Why we leave the arcs showing. In a construction the arcs are your working, just like the lines of working in a calculation. They prove you constructed the shape rather than drawing it by eye. In an exam, rubbing them out can lose you the marks even if the final line is perfect. So: draw arcs lightly, but never rub them out.

2 · A triangle from three sides (SSS)

Suppose you want a triangle with sides 7 cm, 5 cm and 6 cm. A ruler alone isn't enough: you can draw the first side, but where do the other two meet? Compasses answer that question. The third corner must be 5 cm from one end and 6 cm from the other. Each arc shows every point at one of those distances, so the corner is where the arcs cross. This is called SSS (side, side, side). Change the sides and step through again.

Why it works: C is on the first arc, so it is exactly AC away from A. It is also on the second arc, so it is exactly BC away from B. Only the crossing point is on both. (There is a second crossing below the line too; it gives the same triangle flipped over.)

When the arcs won't meet. Try AB = 9, AC = 3, BC = 4. The two short sides together (3 + 4 = 7) can't reach across 9 cm, so the arcs never cross and no triangle exists. For any triangle, the two shorter sides must add up to more than the longest side.

3 · Triangles with a protractor (SAS and ASA)

Sometimes you are given an angle instead of a side. A protractor measures angles in degrees (°). Put its centre cross exactly on the corner, line its 0° line up along the side you have drawn, and count up from 0 on the scale that starts at your line. There are two common cases:

  • SAS (side, angle, side): two sides and the angle between them.
  • ASA (angle, side, angle): one side and the angles at both ends of it.

Protractor mistakes to avoid. Reading the wrong scale (you get 180° − the angle you wanted: 130° instead of 50°); not putting the centre cross exactly on the corner; and in ASA, picking two angles that add up to 180° or more, because then the two lines never meet.

4 · The perpendicular bisector of a line

The perpendicular bisector of a line segment AB is the line that cuts AB exactly in half (bisects it) and crosses it at 90° (is perpendicular to it). You can construct it without measuring anything. Step through, then drag A and B: it works for every line.

Why it works: P is on an arc from A and an arc from B with the same width, so PA = PB: P is equidistant from A and B. The same is true of Q. So the four lengths PA, PB, QA, QB are all equal, and APBQ is a rhombus (a four-sided shape with all sides equal, like a pushed-over square). The diagonals of a rhombus always cut each other in half at right angles. So PQ cuts AB in half, at 90°.

Common mistakes.

  • Arcs too small: the width must be more than half of AB, or the arcs never meet. Drag the width slider below 50% to see this.
  • Changing the width between arcs: tick the "mistake" box. The line is still at 90° but no longer goes through the middle, because PA ≠ PB.
  • Tiny arcs: draw them long enough to cross clearly, above and below the line.

5 · Bisecting an angle

An angle bisector splits an angle into two equal angles. The point where the two arms of the angle meet is the vertex, here called O. Step through, then drag O, A or B to change the angle.

Why it works: the first arc makes OP = OQ. The next two arcs, with the same width as each other, make PR = QR. A four-sided shape with two pairs of equal sides next to each other is a kite (here OPRQ; if all four widths happen to be equal it's a rhombus, a special kite). A kite is symmetrical about the diagonal OR, so OR splits the angle at O exactly in half. Another way to see it: triangles OPR and OQR have all three sides the same (OP = OQ, PR = QR, OR shared), so they are identical by SSS, and their angles at O must be equal.

6 · Perpendiculars from a point and at a point

Two more 90° constructions, both close cousins of the perpendicular bisector. Choose which one:

  • From a point to a line: P is off the line. The perpendicular from P gives the shortest distance from P to the line.
  • At a point on a line: P is on the line and you want a 90° line through it.

Why they work: in both, the first arc makes two points A and B on the line that are the same distance from P. Then the arcs from A and B (with equal widths) find another point Q that is equidistant from A and B. Both P and Q are equidistant from A and B, so the line PQ is the perpendicular bisector of AB, which we already know is at 90°. "From a point" is section 4 with A and B chosen for you by the first arc.

Shortest distance. Of all the paths from P to the line, the perpendicular one is the shortest. Any slanted path is the long side (hypotenuse) of a right-angled triangle, so it is longer. When a question asks for "the distance from a point to a line", it means this perpendicular distance.

7 · Constructing 60°, 30°, 90° and 45°

Some angles can be built with compasses alone, with no protractor at all. 60° comes from an equilateral triangle (all three sides equal, all three angles 60°). 90° is the perpendicular at a point. Bisect them and you get 30° and 45°. Pick one and step through. Drag A or B to turn the starting line.

Why 60° works: AP is a radius of the first arc. AQ is a radius of the same arc, and PQ is a radius of the second arc, which used the same width. So AP = AQ = PQ: triangle APQ is equilateral, and every angle in an equilateral triangle is 180° ÷ 3 = 60°. You can keep going, too: bisect 30° to get 15°, or put 60° next to 90° to make 150°.

8 · Loci: constructions with a purpose

A locus is the set of all the points that obey a rule. You've already met one: the points that are 3 cm from a point O make a circle of radius 3 cm (section 1). The constructions on this page draw two more:

  • Points equidistant from two points A and B lie on the perpendicular bisector of AB.
  • Points equidistant from two lines that cross lie on the angle bisectors of the angles between them.

Discover it yourself: drag the point X around. Whenever it is (almost exactly) the same distance from both, it turns green and leaves a dot. Do the dots make a pattern? Then tick "show the locus".

Worked examples

Construct triangle ABC with AB = 7 cm, AC = 5 cm and BC = 6 cm
Base: with a ruler, draw AB = 7 cm. Label the ends A and B. (Using the longest side as the base keeps the triangle on the page.)
First arc: open the compasses to 5 cm by measuring the gap against your ruler. Point on A, draw an arc above AB.
Second arc: open the compasses to 6 cm. Point on B, draw an arc that crosses the first.
Finish: the crossing is C. Join AC and BC with a ruler. Leave both arcs showing.
Check: measure AC and BC: 5 cm and 6 cm. If you measure angle A with a protractor it should be about 57°.
Construct the perpendicular bisector of a line AB that is 8 cm long
Choose a width: it must be more than half of 8 cm, so more than 4 cm. Use 5 cm.
Arcs from A: point on A, draw an arc above AB and one below (or one long arc).
Arcs from B: without changing the width, point on B, draw arcs that cross the first ones. Call the crossings P (above) and Q (below).
Draw the line: join P and Q with a ruler, extending past both. It crosses AB at its midpoint M.
Check: AM = MB = 4 cm and the angle at M is 90°. Bonus: PM = 3 cm, because triangle AMP has sides 4 and 5 and right-angled triangles with legs 3 and 4 have hypotenuse 5.
Bisect an angle of 70°
First arc: point on the vertex O, any sensible width (say 4 cm). Draw an arc crossing both arms at P and Q.
Middle arcs: point on P, draw an arc inside the angle. Keep the same width, point on Q, draw an arc that crosses it at R.
Bisector: join O to R with a ruler.
Check: each half measures 70° ÷ 2 = 35°.
Construct an angle of 30° without a protractor
Start: draw a line and mark the point A on it where the angle will be.
60° first: point on A, draw a long arc crossing the line at P. Same width, point on P, arc crossing the first arc at Q. Join AQ: angle QAP = 60° (triangle APQ is equilateral).
Bisect: same width again, arcs from P and from Q crossing at R between them. Join AR.
Answer: angle RAP = 60° ÷ 2 = 30°. Leave all four arcs showing.
Construct triangle ABC with AB = 6 cm, angle A = 50° and AC = 4 cm (SAS)
Base: draw AB = 6 cm.
Angle: protractor centre on A, 0° line along AB. Count up from 0 on that scale to 50° and make a dot. Remove the protractor and rule a line from A through the dot.
Second side: measure 4 cm along that line from A (with the ruler or compasses set to 4 cm). That's C.
Finish: join C to B. BC should measure about 4.6 cm.
Exam-style loci: two trees A and B are 6 cm apart on a map. Treasure is buried the same distance from both trees, and 4 cm from tree A. Find where it could be.
"Same distance from both trees" → construct the perpendicular bisector of AB.
"4 cm from A" → set the compasses to 4 cm, point on A, draw a circle (or the arcs that cross the bisector).
Both rules → the treasure is where the circle meets the bisector: two possible places, one each side of AB.
Check: the midpoint M is 3 cm from A, so each place is √(4² − 3²) = √7 ≈ 2.6 cm from M along the bisector.

Quick check

What you've learned

  • A pair of compasses draws points that are all the same distance from the centre: a circle, or part of one (an arc). Every construction uses this one idea.
  • Leave construction arcs showing: they are your working.
  • SSS triangle: draw one side, then arcs of the other two lengths from its ends. The crossing is the third corner. The two shorter sides must add to more than the longest.
  • SAS and ASA triangles: use a protractor for the angles, centred on the corner, reading from 0° on your line.
  • Perpendicular bisector: same width (more than half AB) from both ends; join the crossings. It works because the crossings are equidistant from A and B (a rhombus).
  • Angle bisector: arc from the vertex, then equal arcs from where it cuts the arms; join the vertex to the crossing (a kite).
  • Perpendicular from / at a point: make two points on the line equidistant from P, then find another point equidistant from those two. The perpendicular from a point is the shortest distance to the line.
  • 60° comes from an equilateral triangle, 90° from a perpendicular; bisect them for 30° and 45°.
  • Loci: equidistant from two points → perpendicular bisector; equidistant from two crossing lines → angle bisector; a fixed distance from a point → circle.
  • Biggest mistakes: changing the compass width when it must stay the same, arcs too small to cross, and rubbing out arcs.
