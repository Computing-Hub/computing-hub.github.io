Triangles and Quadrilaterals

Look around you: a slice of toast, a road sign, a kite in the sky, a door, a slice of pizza. Most flat shapes you meet have three or four straight sides. This page teaches you the words mathematicians use to describe them, the symbols they draw on diagrams, and how to name any of these shapes from a few clues. You won't just be told the rules: you'll find them yourself by dragging corners, folding and turning.

1 · The words we need

A polygon is a flat shape made only of straight lines that join up to close in a space. No curves, no gaps. Polygons are named by how many sides they have:

  • A triangle has 3 sides. "Tri" means three, as in tricycle.
  • A quadrilateral has 4 sides. "Quad" means four (a quad bike has four wheels) and "lateral" means side. So quadrilateral literally means "four sides".

To talk about a shape precisely we need names for its parts. We label the corners with capital letters, going round the shape in order. Tap each word to see which part it means.

Two more words describe how two straight lines sit relative to each other.

  • Parallel lines point in exactly the same direction, so they never meet, however far you extend them. Think of railway tracks, or the lines on lined paper.
  • Perpendicular lines meet at a right angle: exactly 90°, a "square corner" like the corner of this screen or a sheet of paper.

Angle between the lines

Common mistakes. "Perpendicular" does not mean "vertical" (straight up and down). Two lines are perpendicular if they meet at 90° to each other, whichever way they are tilted: try the tilt slider at 90° in the panel above.

Parallel lines don't have to be the same length or start in the same place. They just have to point in the same direction.

2 · Reading the marks on a diagram

A drawing can trick your eyes: two sides can look equal without being equal. So mathematicians draw small symbols on diagrams to state facts for certain.

  • Dashes (also called hash marks or ticks) across sides mean "these sides are the same length". Sides with one dash all match each other; sides with two dashes match each other, and so on. One dash and two dashes mean different lengths.
  • Arrows on sides mean "these sides are parallel". Sides with one arrow are parallel to each other; sides with two arrows are parallel to each other.
  • A small square in a corner means "this angle is exactly 90°", a right angle.

Practise reading the marks. Each picture asks one question; your answer gets instant feedback.

Marks beat looks. Exam diagrams often say "not drawn accurately". Only the marks, the labels and any numbers written on the diagram are facts. If a side has no dash, you know nothing about its length, however equal it looks.

3 · Symmetry: folding and turning

Many shapes look balanced. There are two different kinds of balance, and each has its own name.

Line symmetry (also called reflection symmetry): a line of symmetry is a line you could fold the shape along so that one half lands exactly on top of the other, like a mirror. A butterfly has one. Use the "try a fold" button: each orange copy shows what happens if you fold along the orange line. If the copy lands exactly on the shape, that line is a line of symmetry.

Rotational symmetry: imagine tracing the shape, then turning it about its centre. The order of rotational symmetry is the number of times it fits exactly onto its own outline during one full turn (360°). The orange dot is a sticker on one corner, so you can see it really has moved. Every shape fits after a full turn, so the smallest order is 1, which means "no rotational symmetry".

The classic mistake: thinking the diagonals of a rectangle are lines of symmetry. Choose the rectangle and try the diagonal folds: the corners miss. A rectangle that isn't a square has exactly 2 lines of symmetry, both through the middles of opposite sides. Likewise a parallelogram has rotational symmetry of order 2 but no lines of symmetry at all.

4 · Triangles

Every triangle gets two describing words: one about its sides and one about its angles.

Sorted by sides

  • Equilateral ("equal sides"): all 3 sides are the same length. All 3 angles are then equal too, 60° each.
  • Isosceles (Greek for "equal legs"): exactly 2 sides are the same length. The 2 angles opposite those sides, called the base angles, are equal.
  • Scalene: no sides the same length, and no angles the same size.

Sorted by angles

  • Acute: all 3 angles are less than 90° (acute means "sharp").
  • Right-angled: one angle is exactly 90°.
  • Obtuse: one angle is more than 90° (obtuse means "blunt").

The angles of any triangle add up to 180°. You can see why with paper: draw a triangle, tear off the three corners and put their points together. They always make a straight line, and a straight line is 180°. That is also why a triangle can never have two right angles: 90° + 90° = 180° already, leaving nothing for the third corner.

Watch out. An isosceles triangle doesn't have to "stand up" with its odd side at the bottom: turned any way, two equal sides still make it isosceles. The equal base angles are the ones opposite the equal sides, not the angle between them. And "right-angled" describes the angles, so a right-angled triangle can also be isosceles (angles 90°, 45°, 45°) or scalene (for example sides 3, 4, 5).

5 · Quadrilaterals: drag and discover

A quadrilateral can be split into two triangles by drawing one diagonal. Each triangle's angles add up to 180°, so the angles of any quadrilateral add up to 2 × 180° = 360°.

Some quadrilaterals have special properties and special names. Rather than learn a list, explore. Pick a shape, then drag one corner a little.     6 · The quadrilateral family tree

    Here is an idea that surprises many people: a square is a rectangle. Why? A rectangle is any quadrilateral with four right angles. A square has four right angles, so it passes the test. It just has an extra property too (all sides equal). It's like saying every Labrador is a dog, but not every dog is a Labrador: every square is a rectangle, but not every rectangle is a square.

    In the same way, a square is also a rhombus (four equal sides), and both rectangles and rhombuses are special parallelograms (two pairs of parallel sides). Start with a parallelogram below. Make its angle 90° and it becomes a rectangle. Make side AD as long as AB (4 units) and it becomes a rhombus. Do both at once and you get a square. The highlighted boxes in the family tree show every name that fits.

    Tap any box in the tree to read its definition.

    Read the tree from top to bottom: each shape has all the properties of every shape above it that it is joined to, plus something extra. So a square has every property of a rectangle and every property of a rhombus. A rhombus is also a special kite, because its four equal sides certainly include two pairs of equal sides next to each other.

    Which way round? "Every square is a rectangle" is true; "every rectangle is a square" is false. Going down the tree is always safe. Going up isn't.

    Books disagree about one word: some say a trapezium has exactly one pair of parallel sides, others say at least one pair (which makes every parallelogram a trapezium too). That's why the line from trapezium to parallelogram is dashed.

    7 · Guess the shape

    Now use properties like a detective. A mystery shape is hidden. Each clue is a property it has. Guess as soon as you think you know, but if a clue rules your guess out, you'll be told which one. The fewer clues you need, the better. Good strategy: after each clue, think "which shapes are still possible?"

      Worked examples

      A quadrilateral has all four sides 5 cm long and one angle of 70°. Name it and find its other angles.
      Sides first: four equal sides means it is a rhombus or a square.
      Angles: a square's angles are all 90°. This shape has a 70° angle, so it is not a square. It is a rhombus.
      Opposite angles of a rhombus are equal, so the angle opposite the 70° is also 70°. Call each of the other two angles x; they are equal to each other for the same reason.
      Angles in a quadrilateral add up to 360°: 70 + 70 + x + x = 360, so 2x = 360 − 140 = 220 and x = 110. The angles are 70°, 110°, 70°, 110°.
      Check: 70 + 110 + 70 + 110 = 360 ✓
      An isosceles triangle has an angle of 40° between its two equal sides. Find the other two angles.
      Picture it: the 40° is at the top, where the two equal sides meet. The other two angles are the base angles, opposite the equal sides, so they are equal. Call each one b.
      Angles in a triangle add up to 180°: 40 + b + b = 180, so 2b = 140 and b = 70. The angles are 40°, 70°, 70°.
      Careful: if the question had said the 40° was a base angle, the other base angle would also be 40°, and the top angle would be 180 − 40 − 40 = 100°. Read which angle you've been given.
      ABCD is a kite with AB = AD and CB = CD. Angle A = 80° and angle B = 110°. Find angles C and D.
      Which angles are equal in a kite? The line of symmetry goes through A and C (the corners where equal sides meet). Folding along AC puts B on top of D, so angle D = angle B = 110°.
      Angles in a quadrilateral add up to 360°: angle C = 360 − 80 − 110 − 110 = 60°.
      Check: 80 + 110 + 60 + 110 = 360 ✓. Note angles A and C are not equal: they sit on the line of symmetry, and each is folded onto itself.
      How many lines of symmetry does a parallelogram have, and what is its order of rotational symmetry? (Not a rectangle or rhombus.)
      Lines of symmetry: try each possible fold. Along a diagonal, the two halves are the same size but one is flipped the wrong way, so the corners don't meet. Through the middles of opposite sides, the slanted sides lean opposite ways. So there are 0 lines of symmetry.
      Rotational symmetry: turn it about the centre (where the diagonals cross). After a half turn (180°) it fits its outline exactly, and again after 360°. That's 2 fits, so the order is 2.
      Lesson: a shape can have rotational symmetry without line symmetry. They are different kinds of symmetry.
      The diagonals of a quadrilateral are the same length and cut each other in half, but they do not cross at right angles. What is it?
      Diagonals cut each other in half → it is a parallelogram (or one of its special cases: rectangle, rhombus, square).
      Equal diagonals → among those, only rectangles and squares have equal diagonals.
      Not at right angles → a square's diagonals cross at 90°, so it isn't a square. It is a rectangle.

      Quick check

      What you've learned

      • A vertex is a corner, a side joins neighbouring vertices, and a diagonal joins two vertices that aren't next to each other. A quadrilateral has 2 diagonals; a triangle has none.
      • Parallel lines point the same way and never meet; perpendicular lines meet at 90°, at any tilt.
      • On diagrams, matching dashes mean equal lengths, matching arrows mean parallel sides, and a small square means a right angle. Trust the marks, not your eyes.
      • A line of symmetry is a fold line where the halves match. The order of rotational symmetry is how many times a shape fits its outline in one full turn; the smallest is 1.
      • Triangles are equilateral, isosceles or scalene (by sides) and acute, right-angled or obtuse (by angles). Their angles add up to 180°.
      • Quadrilateral angles add up to 360°. Square, rectangle, rhombus, parallelogram, kite, isosceles trapezium and trapezium are each defined by their sides and angles, and have their own diagonal and symmetry properties.
      • The family tree: a square is a special rectangle and a special rhombus; both are special parallelograms; a rhombus is also a special kite. Turning a shape never changes its name.
      • To identify a shape from clues, use each property to rule shapes out until only one fits.
