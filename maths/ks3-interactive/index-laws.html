Index Laws

Three rules that turn multiplying, dividing and powering powers into adding, subtracting and multiplying small numbers. None of them needs memorising: each one is just counting how many copies of the base you have.

National curriculum KS3: use the index laws for multiplication and division of integer powers; GCSE extends to zero, negative and fractional indices

1 · A power is a count

Remember what the index means (see powers and roots): a⁵ is five copies of a multiplied together. Nothing more. Every law on this page comes from writing the copies out, doing something obvious, and counting what's left. Let's agree a picture: each block is one copy of the base a.

2 · Law 1: multiplying powers

a³ × a² is three copies, then two more copies, all multiplied together. That's five copies: a⁵. The indices add. The bases must be the same letter: you're counting copies of one thing.

aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ

Not a × rule. a³ × a² is not a⁶. Three blocks and two blocks are five blocks. If you're tempted to multiply the indices, picture the blocks lined up and count them.

3 · Law 2: dividing powers

a⁵ ÷ a² is five copies on top of a fraction and two underneath. Each copy below cancels one above (a ÷ a = 1). Two pairs cancel, three copies survive: a³. The indices subtract.

aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ

Slide n past m and watch what happens: more copies underneath than on top. That's where zero and negative indices come from, in section 5.

4 · Law 3: a power of a power

(a³)² means "a³, squared": two copies of a³. Each a³ is itself three copies of a, so you have 2 groups of 3, which is 6 copies: a⁶. The indices multiply. This is the one law where multiplying is correct, because you have groups of groups.

(aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ

5 · Zero and negative indices

Law 2 with n bigger than m forces something new. a³ ÷ a³ = a⁰ by the law, but it's also three copies cancelling three copies, which is 1. So a⁰ = 1. And a² ÷ a⁵ = a⁻³ by the law, but drawn out it's two cancelling two with three left underneath: 1/a³. So a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ: a negative index means "on the bottom".

Negative index ≠ negative number. 2⁻³ = 1/8, which is positive. The minus moves the power to the denominator; it doesn't make anything negative. And a⁰ = 1, not 0: zero copies multiplied is the starting point for multiplication, which is 1.

6 · Fractional indices (GCSE)

What could a½ mean? Use Law 1: a½ × a½ = a½+½ = a¹ = a. So a½ is the number that multiplies by itself to give a. That's the square root. Likewise a⅓ is the cube root, and a⅔ = (a⅓)² is "cube root, then square".

a1/n = ⁿ√a     am/n = (ⁿ√a)ᵐ

Order matters for your sanity, not for the answer: root first (small numbers), then power. 8⅔ as (∛8)² is 2² = 4; as ∛(8²) it's ∛64 = 4. Same answer, but one route goes through 64.

8 · Where index laws live

Standard form

(3 × 10⁸) × (2 × 10⁵) = 6 × 10¹³. Multiplying huge numbers becomes 3 × 2 and 8 + 5. Dividing is 8 − 5.

Computing: binary sizes

2¹⁰ bytes in a KiB, 2²⁰ in a MiB. A MiB divided by a KiB is 2²⁰ ÷ 2¹⁰ = 2¹⁰ = 1024. A 2⁵-bit address space squared is 2¹⁰ possibilities… law 3.

Algebra ahead

Differentiation (the first page in this series) rests on xⁿ → nxⁿ⁻¹. Simplifying algebraic fractions like 6x⁵/2x² = 3x³ is Law 2.

Compound growth

1.05¹⁰ × 1.05⁵ = 1.05¹⁵: fifteen years of 5% growth is ten years then five. (1.05¹²)² is 24 years.

Units

Area m², volume m³. Speed m/s = m·s⁻¹; acceleration m·s⁻². Negative indices are how scientists write "per".

Logarithms (A-level)

Logs turn the index laws inside out: log(ab) = log a + log b is Law 1 read backwards. Every slide rule ever built ran on it.

Worked examples

Simplify 4x³ × 3x⁵
Numbers: 4 × 3 = 12.
Letters, Law 1: x³ × x⁵ = x⁸.
Answer: 12x⁸. (Not 12x¹⁵, not 7x⁸.)
Simplify 15a⁷ ÷ 3a²
Numbers: 15 ÷ 3 = 5.
Letters, Law 2: a⁷ ÷ a² = a⁵.
Answer: 5a⁵.
Simplify (2y⁴)³
The power lands on everything inside: 2³ × (y⁴)³.
Law 3 for the letter: (y⁴)³ = y¹².
Answer: 8y¹². The commonest slip is leaving the 2 alone and writing 2y¹².
Exam-style (GCSE): evaluate 27−2/3
Negative: flip it. 27−2/3 = 1 / 272/3.
Denominator 3: cube root. ∛27 = 3.
Numerator 2: square. 3² = 9.
Answer: 1/9. Three moves, each from one law.

Quick check

What you've learned

A power counts copies of the base. Multiplying powers of the same base puts the copies together, so indices add. Dividing cancels copies in pairs, so indices subtract. A power of a power is groups of groups, so indices multiply. Pushing Law 2 past zero gives a⁰ = 1 and a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ; pushing Law 1 into fractions gives a1/n as the nth root. Coefficients are handled with ordinary arithmetic, and each letter is counted separately. When in doubt, draw the blocks.
