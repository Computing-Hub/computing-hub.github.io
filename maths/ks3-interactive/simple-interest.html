Simple Interest

Lend money and you get paid for it; borrow it and you pay. Interest is that payment, worked out as a percentage of the amount per year. With simple interest the same amount is added every year, so the balance climbs in a straight line, and one formula covers everything.

National curriculum KS3: solve problems involving percentage change, including simple interest in financial mathematics. GCSE adds compound interest.

1 · The same amount every year

Put £500 in an account paying 4% simple interest per year. Each year the bank adds 4% of the original £500, which is £20, and it's always £20: the interest already earned doesn't earn anything itself. After 1 year, £520; after 2, £540; after 10, £700. Drag the years and watch the bars grow by equal steps.

2 · The formula

Interest each year is P × R ÷ 100. For T years, multiply by T. That's the whole formula; the letters are the standard ones.

I = P × R × T100

Any of the four can be the unknown. Cover the one you want and rearrange (see the rearranging formulae page): the other three tell you what to do.

Interest or total? Read the question twice. "How much interest does she earn?" wants I. "How much is in the account?" wants P + I. "How much does the loan cost him?" wants I. "How much does he pay back?" wants P + I. Half the marks lost on this topic are from answering the other question.

3 · It's a straight line

Because the same interest is added each year, total against time is a linear graph: y = mx + c with the principal as the intercept and the yearly interest as the gradient. Everything from the linear graphs page applies. Steeper line, higher rate.

4 · Simple versus compound (a preview)

Real savings accounts mostly pay compound interest: each year's interest is added to the balance, and next year's interest is a percentage of that bigger balance. Simple interest adds the same £20 every year; compound adds 4% of a growing number. Over a few years the gap is small; over decades it's enormous. The repeated-percentage-change idea from the previous page is exactly compound interest.

Simple: I = PRT/100
Compound (GCSE): P × (1 + R/100)ᵀ

5 · Where simple interest is used

Short-term loans

Car finance and some personal loans quote a flat (simple) rate: borrow £6000 at 7% flat over 3 years and the interest is 6000 × 7 × 3 ÷ 100 = £1260, paid in equal instalments.

Bonds and savings certificates

A bond paying a fixed coupon each year is simple interest from the holder's point of view: same payment every year, principal back at the end.

Late payments

Statutory interest on an overdue invoice is charged as a daily simple rate: amount × rate × days ÷ 365. T doesn't have to be whole years.

Hire purchase

"£20 a month for 24 months" on a £400 item: you pay £480, so interest is £80 on £400 over 2 years: a simple rate of 10% a year. Working the rate out backwards is the R-unknown case.

Comparing offers

Two lenders, two rates, two terms: compute I for each and compare. The one with the lower rate isn't always cheaper if it runs for longer.

Spreadsheets

=P*R*T/100 in one cell, with P, R and T in their own cells, lets you change any input and watch the interest update: the formula as a live tool.

Worked examples

£1200 is invested at 3.5% simple interest for 4 years. Find the interest and the final amount.
I = PRT/100 = 1200 × 3.5 × 4 ÷ 100 = £168.
Final amount = 1200 + 168 = £1368.
How long does £2000 take to earn £300 at 5% simple interest?
Rearrange for T: T = 100 I ÷ (P R) = 100 × 300 ÷ (2000 × 5) = 30 000 ÷ 10 000 = 3 years.
Check: 2000 × 5 × 3 ÷ 100 = 300 ✓
A loan of £4500 costs £540 in interest over 2 years. What is the simple interest rate?
Rearrange for R: R = 100 I ÷ (P T) = 100 × 540 ÷ (4500 × 2) = 54 000 ÷ 9000 = 6% per year.
Exam-style: Priya invests £600 at 4% simple interest. Sam invests £600 at 4% compound interest. How much more does Sam have after 3 years?
Priya (simple): I = 600 × 4 × 3 ÷ 100 = £72. Total £672.
Sam (compound): 600 × 1.04³ = 600 × 1.124864 = £674.92.
Difference: £674.92 − £672 = £2.92. Small after 3 years; after 30 years it would be about £1226 versus £1320, a gap of nearly £100.

What you've learned

Simple interest adds the same amount every year: a fixed percentage of the original principal. I = PRT/100 gives the interest; the total is P + I. Any of the four letters can be the unknown, and rearranging finds it. Time must be in years to match a yearly rate. Plotted against time, simple interest is a straight line whose gradient is the yearly interest; compound interest, which you'll meet at GCSE, curves upward because each year's interest is a percentage of a growing balance.
