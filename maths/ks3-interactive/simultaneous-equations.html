Simultaneous Equations

One equation with two unknowns has endless solutions. Two equations, both true at once, pin the unknowns down to a single pair. On a graph that pair is where two lines cross; on paper it's found by making one unknown disappear.

National curriculum KS3: solve two simultaneous linear equations in two variables (algebraically and graphically); GCSE adds linear/quadratic pairs

1 · Why one equation isn't enough

x + y = 10. What are x and y? Could be 3 and 7, or 1 and 9, or 12 and −2. Every point on a line satisfies it. Drag along the line: infinitely many answers. Now add a second fact: x − y = 4. Only one point sits on both lines. That's the solution, and the only one.

3 · Elimination: the shopping-bill idea

Before any algebra, here's the whole method in a shop. Two apples and a banana cost 90p. Two apples and three bananas cost £1.50. The apples are the same in both bills, so the difference between the bills must be due to the bananas alone: two extra bananas cost 60p, so one banana is 30p. Subtracting one bill from the other eliminated the apples.

That's elimination. Line the equations up, make the coefficients of one unknown match, then add or subtract so that unknown vanishes. Solve the one-unknown equation that's left, then substitute back to find the other.

4 · Elimination step by step

Choose an equation pair. The solver shows each move; click through so you see why each is made. Pairs are grouped by difficulty: matching coefficients first, then one equation needs multiplying, then both.

Same signs subtract, different signs add. If the matched terms are +3y and +3y, subtracting removes them. If they're +3y and −3y, adding does. Students who always subtract get stuck on the second kind; students who always add get stuck on the first. Look at the signs, then choose. And when you subtract, subtract every term, including the right-hand side.

5 · Substitution: the other route

If one equation already has a lonely letter, like y = 2x − 1, there's a shortcut. You know what y equals, so put that expression in place of y in the other equation. Now there's only x. This is the method you'll need at GCSE when one equation is a curve.

6 · From words to equations

Exam questions rarely hand you the equations. They describe a situation with two unknowns and two facts. The skill is translating. Each fact becomes one equation; the unknowns are whatever the question asks for.

8 · Where simultaneous equations show up

Pricing

Two orders, two totals, unknown unit prices: exactly the shopping-bill example. Businesses back out component prices from bundled invoices this way.

Mixing

How much 20% solution and 50% solution to mix for 10 litres of 35%? Volume equation plus concentration equation, two unknowns. Chemistry, cooking, paint.

Break-even and tariffs

Two phone plans, two cost formulas: where they cross is the usage at which they cost the same. Below it one is cheaper, above it the other.

Computing and graphics

Where does a ray hit a surface? Where do two roads on a map intersect? Every line-crossing in a game engine is a pair of simultaneous equations solved millions of times a second.

Science

Two balanced forces, two circuit loops (Kirchhoff's laws), two reactions sharing a reagent: nature keeps handing you two facts about two unknowns.

Bigger systems

Three unknowns need three equations; a weather model has millions. The method scales (it becomes matrix algebra), but the idea is still "eliminate until one unknown is left".

Worked examples

Solve 3x + 2y = 16 and 5x + 2y = 24
The y-terms match (+2y and +2y), so subtract. (2) − (1): 2x = 8, so x = 4.
Substitute into (1): 12 + 2y = 16, so 2y = 4, y = 2.
Check in (2): 20 + 4 = 24 ✓. Solution x = 4, y = 2.
Solve 2x + 3y = 13 and x + 2y = 8
Nothing matches. Multiply (2) by 2 to get 2x + 4y = 16; now the x-terms match (1).
Subtract: (2x + 4y) − (2x + 3y) = 16 − 13 → y = 3.
Back into (2): x + 6 = 8, x = 2. Check (1): 4 + 9 = 13 ✓. x = 2, y = 3.
Solve y = 2x − 1 and 3x + y = 11 by substitution
Replace y in the second with (2x − 1): 3x + (2x − 1) = 11.
Simplify and solve: 5x − 1 = 11, 5x = 12, x = 12/5 = 2.4.
Find y: y = 2(2.4) − 1 = 3.8. Check: 7.2 + 3.8 = 11 ✓. Fractions and decimals are normal answers.
Exam-style: 3 coffees and 2 teas cost £8.70. 2 coffees and 5 teas cost £11.30. Find the price of each.
Define: c = price of a coffee, t = price of a tea, in pence to avoid decimals.
Equations: 3c + 2t = 870 (1); 2c + 5t = 1130 (2).
Match the c's: (1) × 2: 6c + 4t = 1740 (3). (2) × 3: 6c + 15t = 3390 (4).
Subtract (3) from (4): 11t = 1650, t = 150. A tea is £1.50.
Back into (1): 3c + 300 = 870, 3c = 570, c = 190. A coffee is £1.90.
Check (2): 380 + 750 = 1130 ✓. Both answers are sensible café prices, which is itself a check: an answer like 220.9p would mean re-reading the question.

Quick check

What you've learned

Two unknowns need two independent facts. Graphically the solution is where two lines cross. Algebraically you eliminate one unknown by matching its coefficient in both equations (multiplying if needed) and then adding when the signs differ or subtracting when they match; or you substitute an expression for one unknown into the other equation. Either way you finish by substituting back and checking in both originals. Parallel lines mean no solution; identical lines mean infinitely many. In word problems, name the unknowns first, then turn each fact into an equation.
