Rearranging Formulae

A formula is written for one job: v = u + at gives you v. Rearranging rewrites it for a different job, so it gives you t instead. The moves are exactly the ones you use to solve an equation; the only difference is that the "numbers" are letters.

National curriculum KS3: rearrange formulae to change the subject; GCSE extends to subjects appearing twice, powers and roots

1 · The subject of a formula

The subject is the letter sitting alone on one side. In A = lw the subject is A. If you know l and w, A pops out. But if you know A and l and want w, the formula is pointing the wrong way. You could substitute and solve every time, or you could rearrange once to get w = A/l and use that forever.

3 · Peel the onion

A useful way to plan the moves. Look at the formula from the point of view of the letter you want. What's been done to it, in what order? Each operation is a layer wrapped around it. To free the letter, peel the layers off from the outside in, which means undoing the operations in reverse order.

In v = u + at, the letter t was multiplied by a (inner layer), then u was added (outer layer). So first subtract u from both sides, then divide by a. Click "peel" to remove layers one at a time.

Powers and roots are layers too. If the subject is squared, the last layer to peel is the square, undone with a square root. A = πr²: divide by π, then square-root. Remember both sides get rooted: r = √(A/π), with the whole fraction under the root. And when you take a square root in a rearrangement, write ± unless the context (a length, a speed) rules out the negative.

5 · Checking a rearrangement

You can't "solve and check" because there's no single answer, but you can still check: pick easy numbers, put them into the original formula to get a consistent set, then put the same numbers into your rearranged version and see if it gives the right letter back.

6 · The common slips

Formula → subjectWrongRightWhat went wrong
v = u + at → tt = v − u/at = (v − u)/aThe whole of v − u is divided by a. Brackets or a long fraction line.
y = mx + c → xx = y − c − mx = (y − c)/mm was multiplying, so the inverse is dividing, not subtracting.
A = πr² → rr = √A/πr = √(A/π)Divide by π first, then root the whole thing.
s = d/t → tt = s/dt = d/sMultiply both sides by t first (st = d), then divide by s.
P = 2(l + w) → ll = P − 2w / 2l = P/2 − wDivide the whole of P by 2 (or expand first: P = 2l + 2w).
T = 3 − 2x → xx = (3 − T)/−2x = (3 − T)/2Both are actually equal! But the tidy answer avoids dividing by a negative: 3 − T = 2x.

7 · Where rearranging is needed

Science practicals

You measure voltage and current and want resistance: V = IR → R = V/I. Density ρ = m/V → V = m/ρ to find the volume of an irregular object from its mass.

Programming

Code runs one way. If you have fahrenheit = celsius * 9/5 + 32 and the user types Fahrenheit, you must rearrange on paper and write celsius = (fahrenheit - 32) * 5/9.

Finance

Simple interest I = Prt. A bank quotes the interest and the rate; how long until you earn £500? t = I/(Pr). Rearrange once, reuse for every customer.

Geometry

Circumference C = 2πr, but you measured the circumference of a tree and want the radius: r = C/(2π). Area of a trapezium rearranged gives the height from the area.

Speed, distance, time

s = d/t is the one everyone memorises as a triangle. The triangle is the three rearrangements: d = st and t = d/s. Rearranging lets you throw the triangle away.

Spreadsheets

Goal Seek in Excel is a brute-force rearrangement: "what input gives this output?" Doing the algebra gives an exact formula in a cell that updates instantly instead.

Worked examples

Make x the subject of y = 3x − 7
What's wrapped round x? × 3, then − 7. Peel in reverse: + 7, then ÷ 3.
+ 7 both sides: y + 7 = 3x.
÷ 3 both sides: x = (y + 7)/3. The whole of y + 7 over 3.
Make t the subject of s = d/t
t is on the bottom. First move: multiply both sides by t to bring it up: st = d.
Now t is multiplied by s. ÷ s both sides: t = d/s.
Check with numbers: d = 100, t = 4 gives s = 25. Then d/s = 100/25 = 4 ✓
Make r the subject of V = (4/3)πr³
Layers round r: cubed, then × π, then × 4/3. Peel: ÷ 4/3 (i.e. × 3/4), ÷ π, then cube-root.
× 3 both sides: 3V = 4πr³. ÷ 4π: 3V/(4π) = r³.
Cube root both sides: r = ∛(3V/(4π)). Everything under the root.
Exam-style (GCSE): make x the subject of y = (2x + 1)/(x − 3)
x appears twice, so there's an extra move: get every x-term on one side and factorise.
Clear the fraction: y(x − 3) = 2x + 1 → yx − 3y = 2x + 1.
x-terms left, everything else right: yx − 2x = 3y + 1.
Factorise the x out: x(y − 2) = 3y + 1.
Divide: x = (3y + 1)/(y − 2).

What you've learned

Rearranging a formula is solving an equation whose numbers are letters: do the same to both sides until the letter you want is alone. Plan by looking at what's been done to that letter and undoing it in reverse order, outermost layer first. A letter in a denominator comes up by multiplying through; a squared letter is freed last by square-rooting both sides, keeping everything under the root. Brackets around a whole numerator are not optional. And a quick substitution with easy numbers checks the result.
